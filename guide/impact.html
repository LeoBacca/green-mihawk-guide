Why is 10c Shanks So Broken? Srsly

A Mihawk Guide in OP17 By: Josh “Impact” Graham

Part 1: An Intro to Deck

Slide 3 — What The Deck Is

  • Midrange Deck
  • Rush Finisher
  • Builds Board by developing sticky characters quickly.
  • Finds follow-up plays consistently due to the immense amount of card draw.
  • Aggressive leaning.
  • Really fundamentals heavy deck. Requires good understanding of how to play midrange decks well. Requires good understanding of how to play One Piece well.

Slide 4 — What The Deck Isn’t

  • Not a control deck.
  • Though it is able to control the board and win resource battles if it has to, your primary win condition is dealing damage, not grinding people out of the game.
  • The deck uses freezes to push tempo, not extend the game.
  • This isn’t OP14 style Mihawk where Law and Bepo was used to extend games multiple turns and use boss monsters like Mihawk and Shanks to control the opponent. It is meant to end games quickly.

Slide 5 — Deck’s Strengths

  • Very consistent. Key pieces are searchable with Otama and Perona.
  • Lots of card draw means you will be able to filter towards unsearchable pieces (Vander Decken, Yasopp, Coffin Boat)
  • Deck cheats an unbelievable amount of DON every turn. 4 extra DON between leader and stage.
  • Deck is strong at racing because of Shanks and Luna.
  • Deck is strong at ending games quickly because the excess DON from leader skill lets you develop attackers while attacking for more damage.

Slide 6 — Decks Weaknesses

  • Deck can be weak to early aggression or explosive starts.
  • The deck has a higher than normal brick count (It is a GREEN deck so it is to be expected) so it requires a little bit more micro managing than most decks.
  • Deck can be weak to large characters that are outside of Luna range if you don’t have Vander Decken early.
  • Other than that - no real weaknesses. This is an incredibly overtuned deck.

MulliganSlide 10 — Mulligans

  • Going first
  • Perona, Kinemon, Stage, Otama, YCBMSamurai, Yasopp
  • Going second
  • Perona, Kinemon, Stage, Otama, YCBMSamurai, Yasopp / Oden + Law
  • Cards you are generally looking for:
  • You’re looking for the card draw pieces so you can structure your hand to play the game.
  • You’re often trying to find with your searches / card draw a play for T3 (5 or 6 DON Turn) T4 (7 or 8 DON turn) and T5-6 (Shanks)
  • Because what you need is so wide to play throughout the game, you need to make sure you mulligan for your cards that give you extra cards / looks in the early game.
  • Perona is REALLY Important!! You want to bounce her with Law to continue to find follow up.

Slide 11 — Key Cards

  • Perona, she finds everything
  • Kinemon - helps filter bricks, Law / Samurai target, helps draw towards unsearchable pieces.
  • Shanks. This card DOES EVERYTHING. It’s a finisher, it’s removal, it sets up removal, it sets up Luna. Broken card. Will eventually be pair banned with this leader. Super broken to have a 5 cost 12000 rusher.
  • Law, allows you to double develop. Putting in Yasopp rested doesn’t matter as Yasopp will restand itself.
  • Yasopp is broken. Board control tool in the early game. Very difficult to deal with without hard removal. Also restands your Shanks (important vs Purple or Rocks)

TurnoSlide 12 — Going First Curve

  • Turn 1: Kinemon ideally. Any searcher works but Kinemon costs 1 DON on turn 1 whereas it costs 2 DON on future turns.
  • Turn 2: Perona / Otama -> Samurai. Good turn to Samurai
  • Turn 3: Oden / Yasopp. Develop a 5 that draws a card. Convert additional Samurais / Stage using free DON from your leader skill.
  • Turn 4: Law -> Oden / Yasopp. Double develop. Hand neutral play. Replay Perona if you have it. Find a play for next turn.
  • Turn 5: Law - Yasopp -> Vander Decken if you’re playing a matchup with important 7-8 DON characters (8c Kata/Teach is a big one vs Robin), Law + Oden if you aren’t. This is the ideal turn to cycle searchers and Samurais to find pieces for 10 DON turn.
  • Turn 6: Shanks + Decken and/or Luna, depending on the matchup what you need, but no matter what you need Shanks. You can also use the free DON here to play searchers etc to find more plays if you don’t need to Decken.

TurnoSlide 13 — Going Second Curve

  • Turn 1: Double 1 cost. Be sure to use ones that draw before you search to know what pieces you need.
  • Turn 2: Continue using searchers. This is the first Samurai turn. Make sure to leave a character active for Law if they can actually clear your characters.
  • Turn 3: Law + Yasopp ideally. You can use your leader skill to then rest something, and then pressure the rested character creating a 2 to 0 board lead.
  • Turn 4: Another Law, ideally with Oden on this turn so you can stun a relevant attacker while pushing with your other 6k bodies and excess DON to make good attacks.
  • Turn 5: Shanks. From here on out you’re playing Shanks. Recognize when it is your last turn, then you can play the single Law and Bepo to extend the game 1 more turn if necessary. Make sure to count how many turns you need to actually end the game.

Part 3: Understanding Fundamentals and Deck

Gameplay

Slide 15 — How to Play Deck

  • Use early game to dig for pieces and find more continuing card draw and follow up plays.
  • Use mid game to establish attackers. Use free DON to convert more card draw spells / stages. Try to push a damage every turn with the leader skill.
  • Use the later turns to develop Shanks. Try to time your Lunas with however many turns you think the game will last. Shanks + push a bunch of 7s is also an incredible turn. Why is Shanks a 5 cost 12000 rusher in Mihawk? Unreal.
  • Set up wins by aggressively pushing 7s and getting your opponent into Shanks kill ranges. Without a board you can play Shanks, tap down your stage / searcher and send a 10+12 swing. If it takes multiple Shanks to win, plan towards closing out games via multiple turns with multiple Shanks. This card is so broken lol.

Slide 16 — Pitfalls Playing Deck

  • Not playing aggressively enough. You get three free DON a turn. Use them to push damage.
  • Not cycling bricks. Samurais and Coffin Boats need to get out of your hand aggressively. Kinemon in the later stages of the game can filter bricks on the 8/9 DON Turn or in combination with Shanks. Make sure you don’t stuck on million bricks.
  • Not bouncing Perona with Law. You want to use Perona to find continuation or critical pieces (Billions, Luna) as Perona hits most of the deck.
  • Undervaluing board control. Yasopp their guy and jam it down is a very strong play in the early game.
  • Not getting event counter out of your hand. You will end up spending most of your DON. Make sure to use 1 a turn in the later stages of the game if you have Coffin Boat. If not, look for turns where they’re likely to attack you for damage, and weave them in.

Slide 17 — How to Defend Life as Deck

  • In most matchups you can take fairly aggressively. You need combo pieces and you have the ability to stabilize games at 1-2 life fairly easily when you combine Luna / Billions / Bepo etc.
  • In the mirror - try to counter smaller hits as Shanks will swing too big to counter.
  • Make sure you don’t set yourself up for losses. Don’t take damage you don’t plan on accounting for in future turns. If you think defending a 5 or 6 on this turn means you can take an 8 the next turn (like when they drop Linlin and attack with their triggers) then you should do it if it doesn’t cripple your resources. Think about the whole game, not just this one turn.

Slide 18 — Defending Characters as Deck

  • For the most part - your 6K bodies sticking in the mid game is how you convert wins. Make sure to protect them. Most of the time they’re bigger than your leader. Most of the time - Billion will keep them alive. Make sure to protect your board.
  • You can start letting pieces of your board die when you’re scaling into Shanks, etc in the late game. Calculate if you’re going to need the counter to stay alive vs having an extra 6k body to swing. Ask yourself if that body’s necessary to win. Often times you’ll win by dropping back to back Shanks anyway.

EarlySlide 19 — Early Game Plan

  • Draw cards and find pieces.
  • Search for 5-6, 7-8, 9-10 DON Turns. Make sure you don’t take excess pieces. Make sure you spend card draw and searches to find and complete your curve.
  • Take early damage to draw additional cards as you are a combo heavy deck and are looking for pieces as you have many moving parts.

MidSlide 20 — Mid Game Plan

  • Establish your attackers across 5-9 DON turns. These 6K bodies will convert to 7K swings with the free DON from your leader ability.
  • Cycle any additional searchers with extra DON on the 7-8-9 DON turns. Make sure you’re set up for the late game.
  • Cycle towards Shanks combos and counter events to survive.
  • Pressure your opponent. Make it so you need as few Lunas / Shanks as possible if you can’t find all of them to close the game out. Try to prepare for 1 extra turn than you think you’ll need.

LateSlide 21 — Late Game Plan

  • Use Shanks combo to disable opponent’s boards while pushing damage.
  • Try to end the game in 1 to 2 turns. Shanks is the best finisher the game has ever seen. Use it wisely.
  • Law and Bepo can extend the game an extra turn while developing 1 additional attacker. Good way to punish people who are playing around Shanks (swinging with everything). Law and Bepo cannot extend the game forever anymore due to Shanks + Decken stuff in the mirror so that’s why we don’t build our deck around it anymore, however I’ve really liked the 1 copy.

Slide 22 — How to Filter With Leader

  • As a rule of thumb, try to think at least what you are going to use within the next 2 turns. Don’t try and hold onto non-counter you don’t see yourself playing within the next two turns.
  • When you are playing towards a specific gameplan - try to filter out cards that don’t contribute to this gameplan.
  • When in doubt, discard non-counter before discarding counter. The deck plays a lot of bricks and it can be easy to get stuck on a lot of non-counter.
  • Try to make sure to not get stuck on plenty of events. Make sure you weave them in from time to time to avoid getting clogged up in the late game.

Slide 23 — Using the Leader Skill

  • Important to use the leader skill to push damage. As we are an aggressive leaning midrange deck, it is important to use those extra DON to convert damage since we don’t want to play forever games with our opponents.
  • We also need to make sure we cycle Samurais and Coffin Boats with those extra DON so we don’t get stuck on nothing but non-counter.
  • Calculate how many DON exactly you’ll need for counter events. On average you want to spend 1 a turn, but think about how much damage they can actually do. Count their swings.
  • On the last turn, leave up as many defensive events as you can. Try to end on 3 if you don’t need to Luna.

Slide 24 — How Does Deck End The Game

  • Ends the game with a Shanks + 5 extra DON to swing out.
  • Work your way backwards from there - how do we get in a position to win?
  • We Shanks + Luna / Decken to extend the game while also pushing a damage with a Shanks.
  • Before that - we need them in range of a 2 turn lethal with back to back Shanks.
  • We convert our board in the middle of the game into attacks into the leader to get additional life and cards.
  • Which means we need to build a board while pushing damage.
  • Use leader skill to push damage to get your opponent in 2 turn lethal range with leader swings / your board. End games quickly. Games should end on the 2nd 10 DON Turn. Can extend the game 1 more turn with Law and Bepo if necessary.

Slide 25 — Navigating Towards Win Con

  • Map out the damage you’re dealing each turn.
  • Think about the game as a whole. Which turns you’re dealing which damage. In Pokemon they call it Prize Mapping. In Riftbound they call it Point Mapping. In One Piece we just call it dealing damage.
  • Think about how you’re getting that damage. T2 you’re getting a damage. T3 you’re using free DON from leader skill to push a damage. T4 you’re doing the same thing. Maybe getting cards while doing so. Etc etc.
  • Take an extra turn than you think you’ll need as you’ll often need a turn to get the cards out of their hand once you’ve gotten all or most of their life. It isn’t as easy as get them to 0 and win.
  • Don’t go for risky lethals, most of the time Mihawk can survive their clapback as they have the “free” Rad Beams and Lunas etc every turn. Just use a turn to extract value and force them to go all in against you. Just make sure to put them in those positions when you actually have their attacks covered (count!)

Slide 26 — Turn Around Losing Scenarios

  • Losing scenarios are always winnable because of Shanks.
  • Use Shanks + Luna to get additional turns. Use Shanks to put you closer to the win condition.
  • No matter what, this deck’s win condition is killing the opponent while they’re at 0 with an unblockable swing (Shanks taps down all the blockers) so make sure you’re pushing damage.
  • Try to extend the game additional turns with Law and Bepo, Law + Oden, etc. to get that extra damage with your leader swings.
  • Counter aggressively to buy yourself more time to push more damage.

Part 4: Matchups

Slide 28 — Vs Mihawk - Go 2nd

  • This matchup is about damage. Understanding how to deal damage and how to map damage across multiple turns is going to be the key to victory.
  • Drawing multiple cards in the early game will give you ammo to counter swings while also ensuring you have your curve. Resolving Samurais is very important in making sure you actually can win.
  • This matchup is very different to how it used to be played prior.
  • We play less overall stuns than before. Less Peronas means attacks will go through. When characters get to attack - you need to size the attacks with the goal of dealing damage, not extracting cards.
  • Good numbers to attack - 7000, the deck plays only 8 2000 counters and often puts Otama on the table. 8000 if they don’t have a DON up to get a life or 2 cards. 9000 if they don’t have 8 rested cards. 10000 to attack past Billionfold. 6000 attacks are OK but it is easy for the deck to produce 1000 counters. It is easy for this deck to fail 2K checks.
  • Follow the curve and gameplay instructions listed as this matchup doesn’t deviate much from the plan. Get your opponent to 1 life to set them to 0 with Shanks and freeze their attackers with Luna, then you can do a huge swing with Shanks when they’re at 0 and should kill them.
  • Try to counter smaller swings early. Don’t get stuck on too many events as your free DON from leader can’t always be held up in the late game, especially if you have to freeze their characters with Luna.
  • Luna is very important in the mirror. Try to find the copies with Perona / drawing for them, as the extra turns from Luna save you a lot of counter in the mid-to-late game. Games are won or lost by how many turns you can stall the game with Luna. That’s why we included a Bepo to protect ourselves 1 turn from the 6000 power bodies by becoming an 8000 leader and disabling their Shanks.

Slide 29 — VS Rocks - Go 2nd

  • Rocks is a Slash Leader. Mihawk naturally defends for free, and extracts more counter just on character select. This is not auto-win by itself, but it is the difference of several cards throughout a game.
  • Rocks major weakness is that all of its primary attackers are 7 cost or less, so it is extremely weak to Luna. If Luna + Shanks combo didn’t exist, this matchup wouldn’t be so lopsided.
  • Rocks wins by dealing damage to you really aggressively and trying to “stall out” your Lunas with Captain John. Even if you get them to 0, Captain John can keep them alive. Shanks does tap down the Kyo, but Captain John protects it, so you can’t get to it and Vander Decken. Combined with the “Usopp event” - it can guarantee another turn for the Rocks player. Even just a single Captain John can buy them an extra turn. Account for these extra turns and don’t go “all in” with Luna too early. Bepo is great to buy you an extra turn if they are stalling with John without having to burn a Luna. If you realistically can’t kill them through a Captain John - remove some of their attackers with either Vander Decken or just board control them with the Shanks. For the most part - extra turns benefit Rocks but if you remove all their attackers / their defensive units then they will die anyway.
  • Other than that - you get a lot of free damage because of the fact that your leader is 6000. When you refresh, you will be able to attack them for 7-9000 easily and push through their defenses with your leader.
  • Play around Shiki by not attacking with just 1 6000 power body at a time. Remove Shikis on sight with Vander Decken if they’re not protected by a Kyo. Removing Kyos with Yasopps in the early game is also worth it a fair amount of the time to prevent the snowball. Yasopp does a good job of also being an attacker that “plays around” Shiki by restanding itself.
  • Attack Rocks for 7s when they don’t have Newgate up. Attack them with your leader for 7 if they have Shiki up. Attack for 8s with characters or base numbers if they have Shiki up.

Slide 30 — VS Sabo - Go 2nd

  • Sabo is another type of midrange deck that revolves around planting a lot of 2/5000 power units that provide some sort of value. It punishes you for attacking with individual bodies because of Robin the amount of stats it can put into play can be overwhelming.
  • Yasopp was one of the cards put into the deck originally to help deal with the early characters from Sabo while not losing value. It is meant to board control the 2000 power characters before they have Giants in play. This reduces the amount of pressure coming back at you and gives you more time to extend to your Shanks + Vander combo to deal with their big characters.
  • Try to not put Otama on the table if you can help it, they’re going to 2K check you often with their 6000 power weenies so I don’t want to get caught without. Try to use other searchers, etc to resolve your Samurais.
  • Play for board control so that your Odens are more impactful, stalling their Lokis is ideal as you’re getting the most value stunning 9000 power units that way.
  • For Fun was another inclusion to help deal with Sauls as it is very costly to deal with 7000 power units otherwise. All these tools are here just to buy you extra time as this is one of our worst matchups (still 45-55 btw, and with the techs I think it’s at worst 50-50)
  • Use Yasopps to deal with Zoro ideally so that Vander Decken can land on important characters like Luffy / Loki.
  • If you can remove their only Giant then all their weenies fall immediately after.
  • The Sabo curve is fairly obvious what they’re going to play. Try to leave up DON if possible on certain turns to protect yourself from 7000 power attacks with 1 card. It will be hard to block the 9000 power attacks later so making sure we can stop the 6000s and 7000s while we can is important. Be careful about bleeding life.
  • Very important to make sure you have a reasonable strategy to counterpunch them. You won’t be able to fully stabilize but their defenses are weak so you are often able to 2 turn with Shanks back to back if you can get them to 2. If you aren’t in range of dying (count lethals) - then do your best to set up a 2 turn kill.

Slide 31 — VS Kaido - Go 1st

  • Prior to the Fishman build, Purple ramp decks like Kaido (last set it was Katakuri) were favored as Law and Bepo wasn’t really good enough at stalling out Kaido’s bombs and a Big Mom swing turn was enough to put you far behind.
  • Now with Shanks + Vander Decken combo, your opponents are investing a lot into getting to higher DON totals while you’re pressuring and you’re able to remove their investment while generating tempo. It is similar to Red Roc while you already have a board, except the board is a 12K rush + everything else!
  • Vs Kaido - it is important to understand how to attack to play around their leader skill. Make sure your 6000 power characters attack for 7000. It is a better investment to put the DON on the characters than the leader when trying to get cards. When trying to push for lethal, it is a better investment to put the DON on the leader since the leader doesn’t come with a theoretical -2000 power.
  • Getting Kaido to 0 isn’t that easy as they have multiple heals. Luckily the deck is very bricky and if you’re able to pressure multiple 7000 power swings throughout the game they will fall to low life totals and be forced to heal.
  • Yasopp is very important vs Kaido. It allows you to push with a character without being afraid of them attacking back into it. Later in the game, you also use it to restand Shanks after attacking with him to play around 10-Mom.
  • Take their first so you can develop an extra attacker and slow down their ramp. Ramp decks typically want to go first as they want more turns to use their extra DON.
  • Once you get the Kaido low in life - it is easy to close out the game with a Shanks Vander Decken combo removing a body and setting up lethal. Once they are low in hand, it is easy to use Shanks + all the free DON between the leader and Shanks to attack big into their rested bombs as well. Just make sure to fish out the leader skill with your 6000 power attacks from other units first.

Slide 32 — VS Robin - Go 1st

  • Robin tries to starve you and board control your characters with 3 Sweet Generals. They try to attack you and get you to counter. Best way to counteract this is take the first couple of leader swings while focusing your energy on drawing a lot of cards with Samurai so you can easily get around their leader skill starving you.
  • Use Yasopp to remove their 4000 power bodies so that they don’t have a huge board once they ramp to 10 DON and play Charlotte Linlin.
  • Shanks + Vander Decken and Luna the 4K Bodies is the win condition.
  • Yasopp is very key as it is an attacker they can’t easily remove with 3 Sweet Generals.
  • Pressuring Robin is very key. You can’t let them comfortably play 2 Big Moms as it’ll create a lot of trouble for you if you can’t remove the “buff their team” effect.
  • They don’t really play KO triggers outside of maybe 1-2 Teach so pressuring them with your bodies is good, and go as wide as possible to make sure you have an efficient clock as well.
  • Once you comfortably are in B2B Shanks turns, the opponent will lose. The matchup is not good for Robin with Yasopp controlling them in the early game and Shanks removing all their attackers while setting up counter pressure. Just do what you can to make sure their Moms aren’t getting enough advantage before then that you’re left in a gamestate that isn’t recoverable.
  • Don’t really need to “play around” Gum Gum Giant. Just attack 7s and force it out when possible. Most of the time be prepared for Robin to live a turn longer than you think she will. She is very tanky with Borsa + Giant turns. Make sure that they don’t have established attackers going into their defensive turns and all those turns will do is extend the game that you will inevitably win. If they have attackers and begin to stall is when it becomes a big problem for you.

Slide 33 — VS Enel - Go 1st

  • Establish searchers to remove their 1 cost units without having to invest much in doing so.
  • Use your leader to try and pressure a life every turn ideally. Don’t really expect your board to stick / not be frozen. If something sticks, use it to swing 7000.
  • Oden is great at stalling out big Enels, Save them to try and do so. Yasopp is great at removing their 4 cost units to prevent them from snowballing into you with stuff sticking into another 4c + removal turn.
  • Shanks is great at removing their board or dealing damage. If you can get an Enel to 1 life, back to back Shanks is an easy win. If they are still at high enough life, then using Shanks to deal with a Reiju and using your weenies to clear their 1 costs is a strong turn as well.
  • Make sure to avoid taking too much damage from Enel. Try to counter at least 1 of their swings per turn so that you don’t lose more than 1 life a turn. You’ll need a life buffer to resolve back to back Shanks as Enel is one of the decks that can pressure you efficiently.
  • Shanks + Luna and then putting the Shanks into the Reiju while pushing 7 with lead is a big swing turn. Try to save the pieces, as it is a 4 card combo, but it’ll be expensive and hard to pull off the combo multiple times so try to make sure the turn you do the combo that the Shanks is actually setting up a win on the following turn. Assume your Shanks dies and then you’re sending lethal with your leader and another Shanks.
  • Getting Enel to 0 is basically game over as Shanks send 5 17 is basically always game since they have no +3-4k Event counters.

Deck Identity

LateMidrange With a Rush Finish

Impact defines Mihawk as a midrange deck with a rush finisher.

Against slower control decks, Mihawk is the aggressor:

  • It develops board quickly.
  • It pressures life with repeated 6k and 7k attacks.
  • It forces the opponent to answer several bodies.
  • It uses Shanks to finish before the opponent can stabilize.

Against aggressive decks, Mihawk is more controlling:

  • It uses Yasopp, Oden, Perona, Vander Decken, and Luna to reduce attack count.
  • It clears or freezes the body that makes the opponent’s board dangerous.
  • It creates a board lead, then uses that lead to begin attacking life.

The key distinction is that Mihawk is not trying to do both jobs at once. It changes roles depending on the matchup and current board.

What Mihawk Is Not

Impact is clear that OP17 Mihawk is not the old long-game Mihawk deck.

It is not:

  • A pure control deck.
  • A deck trying to play endless slow Law/Bepo turns.
  • A deck that wants to stall every game indefinitely.
  • A deck that should ignore life pressure just because it plays Luna.

The deck uses stuns to create tempo, not simply to extend the game. It wants to get four or five bodies in play, attack repeatedly with 6k and 7k numbers, and force the opponent to respond to its pressure.

Core Curve

Baseline Curve

Impact’s default curve is:

  • Early one-drops and searchers.
  • 5-DON!! development.
  • 6-cost plus 5-cost development.
  • Another 6-cost plus 5-cost development.
  • Shanks on 10 DON!!.
  • Vander Decken and Luna lines as needed.
  • Law/Bepo as a secondary bomb or turn-extension tool.
  • He describes the core sequence as: One-drops, draw and search, 5, 6 plus 5, 6 plus 5, then 10.

This is not a rigid script. The actual point is to establish enough bodies that your free DON!! can create repeated 7k attacks while you are still developing.

TurnoGoing First

Impact’s preferred going-first early pattern:

  • Play Kin’emon when possible on turn one because it costs only one DON!! then and costs more later.
  • Continue with searchers and one-drops.
  • Use Samurai on the early turns when it converts bodies into more pieces.
  • Develop Yasopp or Oden on the important midgame turn.
  • Use free DON!! to turn 6k bodies into 7k attacks.
  • Use Law plus Yasopp, Law plus Oden, or Vander Decken lines to remove a meaningful opposing character.
  • Spend the later turns on Shanks, Decken, Luna, and follow-up bombs.

TurnoGoing Second

Going second provides a particularly efficient curve:

  • Play two one-drops early.
  • Resolve draws before searches when possible.
  • Keep an active character available for Law if the opponent can clear your board.
  • Develop Law plus a meaningful body.
  • Use leader effect and board attacks to remove the opponent’s first important character.
  • Push 7k attacks on the next turn while the opponent is behind on board.
  • Transition into Shanks turns once the opponent is in one- or two-turn lethal range.

Impact’s central going-second objective is to reach Shanks while the opponent is already under enough pressure that Shanks either puts them to zero or forces them into an impossible defense turn.

Key Cards

Yasopp

Yasopp is the deck’s main sticky attacker and one of its strongest cards.

It provides:

  • A body that is difficult to remove cleanly.
  • Board control against early 2-cost and 5k bodies.
  • A free leader-effect rest target because it stands again.
  • Card draw.
  • Pressure without leaving itself vulnerable in the same way as ordinary characters.
  • A way to attack or remove targets while still enabling future Samurai, search, and leader lines.

Impact is especially high on Yasopp because it can convert a leader effect without the normal downside. It can rest for leader skill, then stand again, giving Mihawk additional flexibility.

Oden

Oden is sticky because it freezes the character most likely to remove or pressure it.

Its strengths:

  • Stops an important attack.
  • Draws into follow-up.
  • Helps keep a body alive by disabling its most natural answer.
  • Is a key stabilizer against decks with one large threat, especially King and Loki lines.
  • Lets Mihawk develop while reducing the opponent’s turn.

Impact identifies Oden as a major priority into Kaido. If Mihawk can freeze King on the relevant 7-DON!! and 9-DON!! turns, Kaido’s pressure collapses quickly.

Perona

Perona is one of the deck’s most important consistency cards.

It:

  • Searches more broadly than Otama.
  • Connects to many continuation pieces.
  • Can be bounced with Law and replayed.
  • Finds Samurais, Lunas, Shanks support, stage, and curve pieces.
  • Gives Mihawk a way to keep cycling through the deck until it has the exact pieces needed.

Impact repeatedly emphasizes that Perona should often be bounced with Law. Replaying Perona is how the deck finds its continuation pieces, especially Lunas and other late-game requirements.

Kin’emon

Kin’emon:

  • Is the most mana-efficient turn-one searcher.
  • Filters bricks.
  • Helps build a board for Samurai.
  • Helps find the non-searchable cards after the searchable Wano core has been removed from the deck.

Impact is clear that Kin’emon is not simply a turn-one body. It is part of the deck’s brick-management engine. When the hand contains cards you do not expect to play, Kin’emon should help convert them into new resources.

Otama

Otama searches a narrower range than Perona.

Impact’s search-order rule: Use the narrowest search first, then use the broader search.

If you have Otama and Perona:

  • Use Otama first.
  • See what the narrow search finds.
  • Use Perona afterward with more information.
  • Take the card that now fits the curve and game plan.
  • Otama is usually more constrained, so resolving it first prevents the broader Perona search from accidentally taking a card that makes Otama worse.

Samurai

Samurai is one of the deck’s core card-advantage pieces.

It:

  • Draws cards.
  • Helps find Shanks, Luna, and other unsearchable pieces.
  • Converts small bodies into hand velocity.
  • Lets Mihawk remove bricks before the late-game combo turns.
  • Helps maintain card quantity in mirrors and longer games.
  • Impact recommends four copies.

He also stresses that Samurai needs board planning:

  • Do not Samurai away all your one-drops if Law needs an active body later.
  • Do not leave yourself without a body for Law.
  • Use draw effects before search effects when possible.
  • If a body is not going to attack, Samurai can be an excellent conversion tool.

Stage / Boat

Stage provides extra DON!! and is an important consistency card.

Impact would like to play more stages if space allowed.

Its functions:

  • Gives additional DON!! for attacks.
  • Lets Mihawk play board and attack for meaningful numbers in the same turn.
  • Helps convert events.
  • Supports 7k attack patterns.
  • Makes Shanks turns even larger.
  • Helps prevent late hands from becoming clogged with events.

The deck’s free DON!! is one of its defining strengths:

  • Leader effect plus stage creates roughly four additional usable DON!! per turn.
  • Shanks can add even more available DON!!.
  • Mihawk can attack for 7k while still developing bodies, something many decks cannot do.

Shanks

Shanks is the deck’s best finisher.

Impact describes Shanks as unusually efficient because playing the rush body and attacking with it can ask for more counter than simply investing all available DON!! into leader.

When the opponent is at zero, Shanks can create an enormous unblockable attack spread.

Shanks does three jobs:

  • Immediate rush pressure.
  • Board control.
  • A setup for Luna.

The standard Shanks goal is:

  • Push the opponent to one or zero.
  • Use Shanks to create a 10k to 12k attack.
  • Freeze the opponent’s board with Luna.
  • Prevent the opponent’s next turn from converting.
  • Play the second Shanks or follow-up bomb to finish.

Vander Decken

Vander Decken is a central OP17 addition.

It helps Mihawk:

  • Remove important midrange threats.
  • Answer 8-cost or larger targets when paired correctly.
  • Kill Shiki, Teach, midrange Katakuri bodies, and similar problem cards.
  • Punish Kaido’s expensive boss turns.
  • Break Big Mom and other large-body lines.
  • Improve the Sabo matchup by removing key Giant bodies.
  • Impact considers Decken a major reason Mihawk’s matchup versus Kaido flipped from difficult to favorable now.

Luna

Luna is one of Mihawk’s main tempo cards.

Luna:

  • Freezes opposing board.
  • Prevents large groups of 6k or 7k bodies from attacking.
  • Makes Shanks turns far more punishing.
  • Creates a pseudo-turn skip when the opponent’s board is rested.
  • Helps Mihawk survive when it is behind.
  • Is valuable in the mirror, Rocks, Enel, and board-based matchups.
  • Impact’s key Luna rule: Use Luna based on how many turns the game has left.

If the game will end in one or two turns:

  • Use Luna aggressively.
  • Freeze the board.
  • Prevent the opponent’s final chance to attack.
  • Finish immediately.

If the game will last longer:

  • A first Shanks can be used more for board control.
  • Save Luna for the turn that actually decides the game.
  • Do not spend all turn-skip tools before you have a lethal clock.

Law and Law/Bepo

Impact likes at least one Law/Bepo.

Law helps:

  • Double-develop.
  • Bounce and replay Perona.
  • Reuse searchers.
  • Deploy Yasopp without leaving it exposed.
  • Create blockers.
  • Extend the game by one turn.
  • Turn a Law/Bepo turn into an 8k leader turn.
  • Punish opponents who are playing around Shanks by attacking with their full board.
  • Law/Bepo is not as important as Shanks plus Luna, but it is an excellent second bomb.

In the mirror, Law/Bepo is better than before because many players now attack with their whole board to play around Shanks. Once they turn their board sideways:

  • Law/Bepo raises Mihawk to 8k.
  • Their 6k bodies lose ability to pressure life.
  • Mihawk can push damage while the opponent’s board cannot convert.
  • Law/Bepo buys the extra turn needed for a second Shanks or a safer lethal.

Rad Beam (Billion Fold)

Rad Beam is a core defensive card.

Impact values Rad Beam because it can defend both:

  • Life.
  • Key board bodies.
  • The correct use is often in the middle game when an opponent tries to clear an important character.

Rad Beam is better than many other events because keeping a 6k or 7k body alive can create repeated attacks and force the opponent to spend another full turn answering it.

Deck Strengths

Consistency

Mihawk has:

  • Otama.
  • Perona.
  • Kin’emon.
  • Samurai.
  • Oden.
  • Yasopp.
  • Stage.
  • Law recycling.
  • Multiple drawing and filtering effects.

The deck often sees a huge percentage of itself over a game. This means it can search for its specific pieces instead of hoping to topdeck them.

Board Reload

Mihawk does not fall behind easily after removal.

For example:

  • Law plus Yasopp generates value.
  • Both cards can replace themselves or create value.
  • If the opponent removes them with a two-for-one effect, Mihawk has still created cards and board value.
  • Perona can be replayed.
  • Oden can draw into a continuation.
  • Samurai turns weak bodies into more cards.

The deck is not invulnerable to removal. It simply makes removal less efficient than it appears.

Free DON!!

Mihawk often gets to both develop and attack.

The deck can:

  • Play a midrange body.
  • Use stage and leader skill.
  • Turn 6k bodies into 7k bodies.
  • Play Samurai or another searcher.
  • Still leave a DON!! for a defensive event.
  • That creates attack patterns other decks cannot match without giving up development.

Flexible Role Assignment

Mihawk can become:

  • Aggro against Teach, slower control, and decks with low counter.
  • Board control against Sabo, Rocks, or wide aggro.
  • Tempo-control against Kaido and yellow Big Mom decks.
  • A race deck when Shanks gives it a one- or two-turn lethal clock.

Deck Weaknesses

EarlyEarly Explosive Starts

Mihawk can lose to unusually strong early starts from:

  • Blue-Green.
  • Sabo.
  • Black Luffy.
  • Other decks that establish several meaningful attackers early.

The key point is that the opponent needs a genuinely strong opening. Ordinary starts are not enough because Mihawk’s stuns, board development, and card draw will usually stabilize.

High Brick Count

Mihawk plays more bricks than many decks, roughly 18 according to Impact.

This is a real weakness. The deck needs disciplined filtering:

  • Use Kin’emon to discard unplayable cards.
  • Use Samurai to draw into active pieces.
  • Play Coffin Boat or stage when appropriate.
  • Do not take redundant bombs from searches.
  • Do not retain a third Law, third Shanks, or extra high-cost card if it will not be played.
  • The deck has enough draw to fix bricks, but only if the player actively manages the hand.

Large Threats Outside Luna Range

Mihawk can struggle if the opponent establishes large characters that cannot be frozen by Luna and Mihawk does not find Decken early.

This is most relevant against:

  • Strong Robin ramp starts.
  • Early Teach into Big Mom progression.
  • Kaido and other large ramp shells.
  • Oversized bosses that arrive before Mihawk has its normal response.

Mihawk can still answer many 8-cost bodies with Yasopp plus Vander Decken and can reuse Decken through Law. But it needs to identify those threats early and search accordingly.

Mulligan Guide

TurnoGoing First

Impact looks for a hand that can actually play the game.

Key keeps include:

  • Kin’emon.
  • Otama.
  • Perona.
  • Stage.
  • Samurai.
  • Yasopp.
  • A functional 5-cost body.
  • A coherent curve with at least two key engine pieces.
  • A hand with Yasopp plus one or two searchers is often a keep as Yasopp itself is unsearchable.

Avoid hands that contain only powerful late-game cards but no early action. A hand full of Shanks, Law, Samurai, and events is not automatically good if it cannot develop bodies or make a functional curve.

TurnoGoing Second

The same searcher and draw priorities apply, but Impact is more willing to keep:

  • A 5-cost body.
  • Law.
  • Two engine pieces plus a path to Oden.
  • Perona hands because Perona can find nearly everything.
  • Law is stronger going second because it can create efficient double-development turns and allows the player to search toward Oden.

MulliganMulligan Rule

The correct mulligan is not based on how many individually strong cards you have.

It is based on whether the hand:

  • Develops early.
  • Finds more cards.
  • Supports Samurai.
  • Has a 5-DON!! play.
  • Has an actual 6-to-10 DON!! curve.
  • Contains enough pieces to execute the plan.
  • Impact’s phrasing is essentially: A hand full of good cards that does nothing is still a bad hand.

Search and Filter Order

Preferred Order

When all options are available:

  • Resolve simple draw effects first.
  • Resolve individual draws before discard-and-draw effects.
  • Use the narrowest search first.
  • Use broader searches after gaining information.
  • Use Samurai after you have maximized information, if board requirements allow.

A standard ideal sequence may look like:

  • Stage first.
  • Kin’emon draw/filter.
  • Samurai draw.
  • Otama search.
  • Perona search.

The exact sequence changes when Samurai needs specific bodies or Law needs a character. However, the principle remains: More information before a major search produces better decisions.

Search Priorities

Search for the card that completes the next two turns.

Do not take:

  • A third 5-cost.
  • A third Law.
  • A redundant Shanks.
  • A brick you do not plan to play.
  • An event that will be stuck in hand later.

Take:

  • The first body that creates your next development turn.
  • Samurai if card velocity is the limiting resource.
  • Perona when you need broad access.
  • Oden when the matchup requires a freeze.
  • Yasopp when early board control is the win condition.
  • Luna when the next two turns are likely the finish.

Midgame Play

Establish Attackers

From 5 to 9 DON!!, Mihawk should focus on getting attackers into play.

The purpose of 6k bodies is to become 7k attackers through free DON!!. Do not waste the deck’s free DON!!.

Impact’s core rule: If one DON!! turns a 6k body into a 7k attack and that attack demands two cards worth of defense, use it.

Mihawk has an advantage because it can make these attacks while still developing board.

Cycle Bricks Before Combo Turns

Mihawk’s 10-DON!! and later turns often consume all available DON!!.

This means the 7-DON!! and 9-DON!! turns are vital for:

  • Playing Samurai.
  • Playing Boat.
  • Replaying Perona.
  • Using Kin’emon.
  • Converting events.
  • Removing bricks.
  • Finding Shanks, Decken, Luna, and defensive resources.
  • If the player holds too many events or bricks until the late game, those cards can become unusable because Shanks plus Decken plus Luna consumes the entire turn.

LateBoard Control Before Lethal

Mihawk should not blindly send every attack face just because it is a Luna deck.

Early board control creates the attack advantage that later makes Shanks lethal.

Example:

  • Law plus Yasopp removes an opposing Yasopp.
  • The opponent must develop into your existing board.
  • Their new body cannot attack immediately.
  • You retain a multi-character board.
  • You freeze another body.
  • You now attack four or five times while they attack once or twice.

This is the foundation of Mihawk’s pressure. You may sacrifice one attack to kill a body, but you permanently deny that body’s future attacks and create a superior board state.

Late Game and Lethal Planning

Work Backward From Shanks

Mihawk’s endgame is usually:

  • Shanks creates a huge rush attack.
  • Luna removes the opponent’s ability to counterattack.
  • The opponent is put at zero or one.
  • A second Shanks, Law/Bepo, or board attack finishes.
  • Impact recommends damage mapping across the entire game.

Ask:

  • How will I take their first life?
  • How will I take their second and third life?
  • What attack puts them into Shanks range?
  • Does the first Shanks put them at zero?
  • If not, can I survive one more turn?
  • Does the second Shanks kill?
  • Do I need Law/Bepo, Bonney, or Luna to buy that extra turn?

LateDo Not Ship Bad Lethals

Mihawk is unusually good at playing one additional turn.

If the lethal is uncertain and you are not dead next turn:

  • Do not all-in.
  • Use Rad Beam, Luna, Law plus Bonney, Law plus Oden, or Law/Bepo to survive.
  • Force cards from the opponent’s hand.
  • Preserve board.
  • Set up a cleaner lethal next turn.

Impact’s rule:

  • If you can afford one more turn, take one more turn.
  • This deck has enough draw, counter, stuns, and defensive options that it frequently wins by forcing the opponent to attempt an all-in first.

Mihawk Mirror

Matchup Identity

The OP17 mirror is primarily a damage-mapping and tempo matchup.

The old mirror was more directly about Law/Bepo chains and prolonged leader-size wars. The current mirror is more midrange-focused:

  • Develop bodies.
  • Attack for real numbers.
  • Use Shanks and Luna to skip the opponent’s turn.
  • Put the opponent to zero before they can do the same.
  • Preserve enough life and counter to survive the first major Shanks turn.
  • Impact prefers going second.

TurnoWhy Going Second Is Strong

Going second gives:

  • Better development curve.
  • Earlier access to the first major Shanks turn.
  • Better ability to use Luna first.
  • Natural initiative in the bomb exchange.
  • More consistent ability to convert the board before the opponent’s bomb turn.

Attack Numbers

Impact’s key mirror attack thresholds:

  • 7k: Important because the deck plays fewer 2ks than it appears to.
  • 8k: Strong when the opponent has no open DON!!, often forcing life or two cards.
  • 9k: Useful when the opponent cannot efficiently use Billion Fold or equivalent defense.
  • 10k+: Usually less efficient than splitting into 7k and 8k attacks unless specifically attacking past an event threshold.
  • He prefers spreading DON!! into multiple 7k and 8k swings instead of one giant attack in most mirror spots.

Life Management

Unlike older Mihawk mirrors, players should counter more of the smaller attacks.

Why:

  • Shanks creates a 12k attack on the bomb turn.
  • Mihawk at zero has limited reliable survival.
  • Law can be answered.
  • Blockers are not dependable.
  • A player needs a life buffer before the Shanks turn.
  • Impact’s general mirror goal is to defend enough 6k to 8k attacks in the midgame that Shanks does not put you to zero too early.

However, do not counter with the only card that makes your next turn work. The player must consider the entire game, not merely the current attack.

Stun Bonney

Stun Bonney is a major mirror tech.

It can:

  • Prevent the opponent’s Shanks turn.
  • Buy an entire tempo turn.
  • Let the player going first become the first Shanks player instead.
  • Be reused with Law.
  • Stop the opponent from reaching its full 10-DON!! line.

Impact views Bonney as especially valuable because it can be replayed with Law. One Bonney can potentially deny multiple Shanks turns if the player has enough Law access.

Law/Bepo in the Mirror

Law/Bepo is still good as a secondary bomb.

Use it when:

  • The opponent has attacked with its full board to play around Shanks.
  • You need to become an 8k leader.
  • You need to deny several 6k bodies.
  • You need one extra turn before the second Shanks.
  • You need to prevent the opponent’s board from converting.

The goal is not to build the deck around Law/Bepo. Shanks plus Luna is the primary endgame. Law/Bepo is the flexible follow-up that can buy time or force a final damage sequence.

Mirror Mistakes

Common errors:

  • Attacking for numbers that only extract 1ks rather than force life or 2ks.
  • Taking too many small hits and entering Shanks range at zero life.
  • Saving too much DON!! for events instead of pushing 7k attacks.
  • Failing to cycle Perona and Samurai to find Luna.
  • Playing the bomb turn too early before the opponent is in one- or two-turn lethal range.
  • Holding Rad Beam until it becomes unusable because Shanks plus Luna consumes all DON!!.
  • Ignoring Stun Bonney timing.
  • Losing to a stronger player’s tempo plan because you did not map the opponent’s Shanks turn.

Mihawk vs Rocks

Matchup Assessment

Impact considers Mihawk very strong into Rocks.

Rocks is a Slash leader, which gives Mihawk a major mechanical advantage. Mihawk naturally handles 5k leader swings efficiently and can use its own leader power and defensive events to reduce Rocks’ pressure.

Why Mihawk Is Favored

Rocks’ core attackers are often 7-cost or lower, making them vulnerable to Luna.

Mihawk can:

  • Freeze Rocks’ board.
  • Use Shanks to disable key attackers.
  • Use Vander Decken on unprotected Shikis.
  • Use Yasopp to remove Kyo early.
  • Attack Rocks’ life with 7k leader swings.
  • Prevent Newgate and Shiki from converting hand into defensive value.
  • Keep Rocks from using Captain John as a real comeback plan.

Rocks Threats to Respect

Newgate

Newgate does more than provide 3k counter conversion.

It also allows the Rocks leader to attack for 8k without normal DON!! investment.

Removing Newgate:

  • Removes a defensive engine.
  • Removes a large attack modifier.
  • Prevents Rocks from turning extra hand cards into pressure.
  • Makes future attacks much easier to defend.

Shiki

Remove Shiki whenever possible, especially when it is not protected by Kyo.

Shiki:

  • Controls board.
  • Removes key Mihawk attackers.
  • Turns Rocks’ hand into meaningful defense.
  • Can snowball if left alive.

Captain John Plus Usopp Event

This is the key surprise line.

Rocks can use Captain John plus the Usopp event to gain an extra turn and potentially threaten an unblockable finish.

Mihawk should:

  • Do not overcommit Luna too early.
  • Track whether Rocks can protect Captain John.
  • Remove Rocks’ other attackers so Captain John cannot become a real win condition.
  • Use board control when Rocks is at zero but still has Captain John potential.

How to Play the Matchup

Go second.

Develop efficiently.

Use Yasopp to remove Kyo early when possible.

Use Decken on Shiki if Kyo is absent.

Attack Rocks for 7k when Newgate is not active.

Attack with 8k or baseline character numbers when Shiki is active.

Prioritize board control over a reckless all-in.

Remove Newgate and Shiki when the opportunity is efficient.

Use Luna to freeze the 7-cost board.

Do not allow Captain John to create an extra-turn win condition.

Impact compares this to old Kid-style board control: Captain John may keep a unit alive, but if Rocks has no other attackers, it cannot actually make progress.

Mihawk vs Sabo

Matchup Identity

Sabo is a midrange deck that:

  • Develops multiple 2-cost and 5-cost utility bodies.
  • Uses Giant effects to make those bodies attack as 6ks.
  • Creates early board pressure.
  • Transitions into Luffy rushes and larger boss turns.
  • Uses graveyard resources for continued value.

Mihawk would normally struggle against a wide midrange board. Yasopp changes the matchup.

Key Strategy

Mihawk should control Sabo’s early utility bodies before they become 6k attackers.

Use Yasopp to:

  • Remove early 2-cost bodies.
  • Kill Zoro.
  • Deny the body that makes the Sabo board work.
  • Create time to find Shanks, Decken, and Luna.

Use Oden to:

  • Freeze Loki.
  • Stop the biggest attacker.
  • Reduce the opponent’s attack count.
  • Buy a turn for Mihawk to develop or push life.

Use Vander Decken to:

  • Remove Giants.
  • Kill Saul or other important 12-cost-enabling bodies.
  • Stop Sabo from converting its board.
  • Deal with larger characters after Yasopp handles smaller threats.

Attack and Defense

Sabo is weak defensively. Mihawk can frequently two-turn it with back-to-back Shanks if Sabo is at two life.

The key question is whether Mihawk can die this turn.

If Mihawk is not in lethal range:

  • Send as much damage to life as possible.
  • Force Sabo low.
  • Use Shanks next turn to finish.
  • Do not overfocus on clearing every body.

If Mihawk is in danger:

  • Leave a DON!! for Billion Fold or another defensive event.
  • Use Oden to stop the largest attacker.
  • Use Yasopp to remove a smaller threat.
  • Keep Otama in hand when possible because Sabo frequently 2k-checks Mihawk and Otama may be needed as counter.

When to Starve

Mihawk can starve Sabo at four life if it wins board early.

A board-control line might look like:

  • Yasopp removes their first character.
  • Law plus Yasopp removes the next.
  • Mihawk has three bodies to their one.
  • Oden freezes their remaining attacker.
  • Mihawk attacks face because Sabo can no longer rebuild or race.

If Mihawk is still fighting for board, it should keep fighting for board. If it wins board clearly, it should kill Sabo.

Important Sabo Rule

If Mihawk removes Sabo’s Giants, its smaller units lose much of their value.

This is often the simplest route:

  • Yasopp handles Zoro and early utility bodies.
  • Vander Decken handles the Giant.
  • Oden stalls Loki.
  • Luna skips the board’s conversion turn.
  • Mihawk pushes the now-weak board out of the game.

Mihawk vs Kaido

Matchup Assessment

Impact sees Kaido as much more manageable now because Vander Decken answers its expensive boss turns.

In previous formats, purple ramp decks could outscale Mihawk. Law/Bepo was not enough to stall the large bodies, and Big Mom-style cards could answer Mihawk’s bomb.

Now, Mihawk can often answer a 10-DON!! Kaido body with a much cheaper Decken-based interaction line.

TurnoGoing First

Impact recommends going first into Kaido.

The goal is to disrupt Kaido’s preferred curve:

  • Prevent or delay the easy King ramp line.
  • Establish an extra attacker.
  • Make Kaido spend its DON!! responding rather than ramping.
  • Keep it from reaching the point where it has more bosses than Mihawk has answers.

Ramp decks want extra turns to exploit extra DON!!. Taking first denies them some of that time.

Game Plan

Establish board early.

Pressure Kaido before it gets several boss turns.

Use Shanks plus Vander Decken to answer major threats.

Use Oden to freeze King.

Force Kaido to choose between developing, healing, and removing your board.

Attack in numbers that overload Kaido’s available DON!!.

Expect an extra turn because Kaido can heal.

Continue pushing board plus life rather than waiting indefinitely.

Fishing for Kaido Leader Effect

When Kaido has leader defense available, attack sizing can manipulate its choices.

Swing 6k if you want Kaido to use leader effect or take the hit.

Swing 7k if you want Kaido to use leader effect and still give a card.

Use a sequence of smaller attacks to force the leader effect before committing Shanks.

Once the leader effect is spent, Shanks can attack a large body for a huge number.

A representative line:

  • Attack 6k, 6k, 6k.
  • Force or bait Kaido’s leader effect.
  • Play Shanks.
  • Rest or target the large Kaido body.
  • Put large DON!! onto Shanks.
  • Attack the boss body for 17k or force Kaido to give multiple cards.
  • Either the body dies or Kaido loses most of its hand and life.

Important Rule

Do not give Kaido unlimited time.

If Kaido reaches repeated boss development turns, it will eventually have more threats than Mihawk can answer. Mihawk must pressure, answer the first bomb efficiently, and make Kaido develop into an already-established board.

Mihawk vs Robin and Yellow Big Mom Decks

Matchup Identity

Robin tries to starve Mihawk through a banish leader, build a board, use Sweet Generals for control, then stabilize with Big Mom and Giant defense.

Mihawk is favored when it prevents Robin from developing a meaningful attacker base.

TurnoGoing First

Impact prefers going first into Robin.

The goal is to:

  • Interrupt Robin’s ramp and Big Mom progression.
  • Establish an extra attacker.
  • Use Yasopp to remove 4k bodies.
  • Keep Robin’s board narrow when Linlin enters.
  • Prevent Robin from comfortably playing two Big Moms.

EarlyEarly Plan

Mihawk should:

  • Take the first few banish hits when safe.
  • Search for card draw and continuation.
  • Use Yasopp to clear Robin’s 4k body.
  • Use Oden to freeze the biggest relevant attacker.
  • Keep Robin to zero or one meaningful attacker going into its Linlin turn.
  • Pressure life before Robin can establish repeated Big Mom healing.
  • Robin’s banish plan is less dangerous if Mihawk uses searchers and draw to out-card the starvation plan.

Stun Bonney

Stun Bonney is extremely strong if it takes Robin off the 10-DON!! Big Mom turn.

If Mihawk denies that key turn:

  • Robin often has no comparably productive replacement.
  • Mihawk gains another development turn.
  • Robin’s board remains too narrow.
  • Mihawk can push life before healing and Giant defenses come online.
  • The exception is more flexible Pudding-style yellow builds, which may have alternate plays.

Borsalino Plus Giant

Impact identifies Borsalino plus Giant as Robin’s strongest defensive structure.

To beat it:

  • Ensure Robin does not have established attackers going into the defensive turn.
  • Go wide.
  • Force Giant through repeated 7k attacks.
  • Use Shanks to remove the big body when possible.
  • Make Robin spend its whole turn staying alive rather than building pressure.

If Robin is defending but has no meaningful board, it is not winning. It is only delaying the game.

Linlin Choice

When Linlin offers discard versus heal, Impact generally prefers the discard.

Exception:

  • Let them heal only if the random discard could hit a card that completely ruins the current game plan.
  • If they’re low on hand, making it heal becomes stronger because the extra discard is a real cost.
  • If Mihawk has lethal, never give the heal.

Mihawk vs Enel

Matchup Identity

Mihawk versus Enel is more comfortable in OP17 because Mihawk now has stronger board control and Shanks-based lethal lines.

The plan is:

  • Control Enel’s one-drops.
  • Use Yasopp to remove midrange bodies.
  • Use Oden to stall large Enels.
  • Push life every turn with leader power.
  • Do not let Enel develop a wide board.
  • Use Shanks and Luna to remove both large and small threats.

Key Cards

Yasopp

Yasopp removes Enel’s midrange bodies before they snowball.

A key Enel win pattern is:

  • A body sticks.
  • Enel develops another 4-cost card.
  • It removes or pressures Mihawk’s board.
  • It begins to snowball.
  • Yasopp interrupts that sequence by killing the 4-cost before Enel can build around it.

Oden

Oden is excellent against large Enel characters. Use it to stall a major body and reduce Enel’s attack count.

Shanks and Luna

When Shanks is played, Mihawk can often clear or disable Enel’s board:

  • Shanks handles Reiju or another major body.
  • Luna freezes the remaining one-drops and attackers.
  • Searchers can be used to clean up the smaller board.
  • Enel can be left with no meaningful attacks except Rush Shura.
  • This is much different from older Mihawk, where Enel could force a pseudo-race through removal and board pressure.

LateSecret Enel Lethal Pattern

If Enel is at one life and Mihawk has two Shanks:

  • Play the first Shanks.
  • Send the board and leader at life.
  • Put Enel at zero or exhaust its resources.
  • Use the second Shanks next turn for the kill.
  • Mihawk’s leader can push large numbers every turn, so getting Enel low is realistic if the Mihawk player does not waste attacks.

Hand-Rip Law

Mihawk should try to play around Enel’s hand-rip Law when possible.

Counter a small attack if it keeps you out of hand-rip range.

Use event counters on early minor attacks if doing so protects key combo pieces.

Do not hold low-value cards such as Decken or Kawamatsu if they can simply be used as counter.

Preserve enough life and hand that Enel cannot hand-rip repeatedly.

The goal is not to prevent every Law. The goal is to prevent consecutive Law turns from removing all of Mihawk’s critical pieces.

Common Mistakes

Not Attacking Aggressively Enough

Mihawk receives free DON!! every turn. Use it.

Common mistake:

  • Leaving DON!! open for events that are not needed.
  • Attacking 6k when 7k is available.
  • Failing to pressure life.
  • Waiting too long to put the opponent into Shanks range.

Correction:

  • Convert free DON!! into 7k attacks.
  • Push damage every turn when board state allows.
  • Use events only when they preserve a critical body or prevent real lethal.

Holding Bricks

Common mistake:

  • Keeping a third Law.
  • Keeping a third Shanks.
  • Holding redundant 5-cost bodies.
  • Holding events until they become unusable.
  • Failing to use Kin’emon and Samurai.

Correction:

  • Plan the next two turns.
  • Discard non-counter cards you will not play.
  • Use Samurai to cycle pieces before Shanks turns.
  • Use Boat and events before the late game if they will be stuck later.

Not Bouncing Perona

Perona should often be bounced and replayed with Law.

Why:

  • It finds continuation.
  • It finds Luna.
  • It finds Shanks support.
  • It finds Samurai.
  • It filters the deck into the cards that matter.
  • A Law turn that does not reuse Perona must have a clear reason to be better.

Overvaluing Face Pressure

Mihawk is aggressive, but board control still matters.

If Yasopp can kill a key early body:

  • Use it.
  • The opponent must develop into your board.
  • That new body cannot attack immediately.
  • You gain a permanent attack advantage.

Mihawk does not win by blindly attacking life every turn. It wins by establishing an attack advantage and then converting that advantage into life pressure.

Holding Events Too Long

If you do not have stage, late Shanks turns can consume all DON!!.

Before committing to a Shanks plus Decken plus Luna turn, ask:

  • Will I need these Rad Beams next turn?
  • Do I have a chance to use them now?
  • Is this the last turn I can convert event counter before the full-DON!! bomb turn?
  • Do I need to save natural counter for the late game?
  • Do not die with several event counters trapped in hand.

Final Tournament Checklist

Before registering Mihawk, players should know:

  • Their exact mirror die-roll preference.
  • Their planned number of Shanks, Law/Bepos, Lunas, Bonneys, and defensive events.
  • Which slots are flex cards and what matchups they improve.
  • Their answer to Sabo’s early board.
  • Their plan to deny Rocks’ Newgate and Shiki board.
  • Their Kaido line for King and the first 10-cost boss.
  • Their Robin plan for Borsalino plus Giant.
  • Their Enel plan for one-drops, 6c Enels, and hand-rip Law.

During every match, players should ask:

  • What is my win condition this game?
  • Does this turn need to develop board, control board, pressure life, or survive?
  • Which cards in hand do I realistically play in the next two turns?
  • Which cards are bricks and should be filtered?
  • How many attacks can the opponent actually send next turn?
  • Can I afford one more turn instead of taking a risky lethal?
  • Does my Shanks turn put the opponent to one or zero?
  • Can I Luna the board on the turn it actually matters?

Final Takeaways

Mihawk is an aggressive midrange deck, not a passive control deck.

Shanks plus Luna is the deck’s defining OP17 endgame.

Yasopp, Oden, Perona, Kin’emon, Samurai, and stage make the deck exceptionally consistent.

The mirror rewards damage mapping, 7k attack sizing, Stun Bonney timing, and preserving life for Shanks turns.

Rocks is favorable if Mihawk controls Newgate and Shiki while respecting Captain John lines.

Sabo is controlled by Yasopp, Oden, Decken, and accurate race decisions.

Kaido must be pressured before it establishes multiple boss turns.

Robin must not be allowed to settle behind repeated Big Mom and Giant turns.

Enel’s board must be controlled before it snowballs, then finished with Shanks pressure.

The biggest Mihawk mistake is leaving free DON!! unused instead of turning board into meaningful attacks.

The best Mihawk players will not merely know their cards. They will know how much damage they need to deal before Shanks, which bodies they must remove, which attacks they can safely take, and when to turn a favorable board into a two-turn kill.

Big Picture

Going into Nationals / the Flame Flame Fruit weekend there are three or four decks you can really consider: Mihawk, Robin, Enel and RG Luffy-Ace; "everything else is kind of mid". Mihawk and Robin are "that little bit better than everything else", Enel and Luffy-Ace are right there. Rocks beats everything that beats Enel and Robin, but loses to Mihawk. Pick the one you are most comfortable with, not the one the meta tells you to FOMO into.

The one real change of the week is the rest spell — I Never Bother to Remember the Faces of Trash (OP14-038, the "trash event") — which "became more popular because it did very well and got a lot of good publicity over the last week". A free tap-and-trade tool paid by the leader that turns the early-game decks that used to prey on Mihawk (Sabo, Enel) into good matchups and makes the deck a board-control deck first, aggro second. "Sabo has gotten a lot worse this week" because of it. Mihawk overperformed last weekend: 5 of the top 8 at the big regional, 11 in the top cut vs 6 Robin and 5 Enel.

1. The Rest Spell: Mihawk Becomes a Board-Control Deck

  • What it doeslets you tap your searchers / 1-drops and trade into their things; a keep-away game against Sabo and Enel, the two matchups that were tougher.
    • Before, you had to wait for Yasopp to deal with a T2 body; now you answer it on turn 2.
    • It is a spell that costs nothing because the leader pays for it: "spells cost nothing because your leader lets you do it for free".
  • The exchange math on the early trade: you used one card from hand that replaced itself → zero cards spent; they either lose the character (lose a card) or counter and lose two cards to protect it.
  • Keep removingspell + Yasopp + Law + Yasopp again, every turn, until you have won the board. You are full-starving Enel and Sabo until the board is won.
    • Once the board is won: one turn to pull cards out of their hand, one turn to take a lot of life, next turn kill — or life first, then cards, then kill.

4. Playing From Behind: Find the Win Condition in Your Hand

  • First look at your cards and decide what your win condition is. "My hand is just all those cards, that is the game I'm trying to play."
  • You are not just playing the cards in your hand but the cards in your deck. If the plan is play Shanks, swing out, top-deck another Shanks, top-deck another Shanks — "that's just how it is".

IMPACT OP17 META Q&A

Deck Selection, Meta Updates, Matchup Theory, and Tournament Preparation

Session Overview

This session is Impact responding to Dojo questions while updating his view of the OP17 metagame shortly before the weekend's major events. The conversation centers on Mihawk, Robin, Enel, Sabo, Rocks, Luffy Ace, deck selection, new tech choices, and how to prepare mentally for long tournament days.

The largest update is that Mihawk’s new rest-event build has improved its early-game control matchups substantially. Impact believes the deck can now remove early Sabo and Enel bodies earlier and more efficiently than before, which makes Sabo a much less attractive registration than it was in prior weeks.

His broad current hierarchy is:

  • Mihawk
  • Robin
  • Enel
  • Red-Green Luffy Ace

He sees these as the most realistic high-level event choices. Rocks remains extremely powerful into much of the field, but its Mihawk matchup is too severe for Impact to recommend it if Mihawk remains popular.

Meta Update

Mihawk Has Improved

Impact believes Mihawk became better because the new rest spell gives the deck earlier, cheaper board control.

Previously, Mihawk often had to wait until Yasopp or later interaction to answer early characters. That gave decks such as Sabo and Enel opportunities to establish an early board and leverage it before Mihawk’s main control tools came online.

Now, Mihawk can:

  1. Play stage.
  2. Develop a one-drop.
  3. Use the rest spell on the following turn.
  4. Rest an opposing early body.
  5. Attack it for a meaningful number.
  6. Draw or filter while trading favorably.

This changes the exchange dramatically.

If Mihawk uses one card that replaces itself and removes the opponent’s character:

  • Mihawk often loses no meaningful hand resources.
  • The opponent either loses the character outright or spends multiple cards defending it.
  • Mihawk gains time to develop Yasopp, Oden, Perona, Law, and Shanks.

Impact sees this as especially important against Sabo and Enel, which previously gained a lot of equity from early board development.

Sabo Is Worse This Week

Impact is much lower on Sabo than he was previously.

Why:

  • Mihawk’s rest spell gives early answers to Sabo’s key 2-cost development.
  • Mihawk can now rest and remove a Sabo body before it becomes a 6k attacker.
  • Yasopp can remove the next body.
  • The rest spell can later remove Saul or another important Giant.
  • Sabo loses much of its early pressure if its first two meaningful bodies are removed.

Impact’s conclusion is blunt: Registering Sabo into the current Mihawk-focused field is likely to be a bad experience.

Sabo can still win when it curves perfectly or Mihawk fails to find interaction, but the matchup has shifted away from the early OP17 Sabo advantage.

Choosing a Deck

Mihawk vs Sabo

Impact’s answer to the question “Why register Sabo with practice instead of Mihawk with OP16.5 experience?” is effectively that there is little reason to choose Sabo right now unless a player has extraordinary comfort with it and expects a very specific field.

Mihawk is the better general registration because:

  • It remains highly consistent.
  • It rewards strong fundamentals.
  • It gained new tools against previous difficult matchups.
  • It has a good plan into Sabo, Enel, Robin, and Rocks.
  • The mirror is a fundamentals matchup where OP16.5 experience still transfers.
  • It is less dependent on the opponent failing to interact with its board.

Play the Deck You Know

Impact’s event recommendation is not to chase every new list or switch decks because of fear of missing out.

For a major event:

  • Pick the deck you understand.
  • Know your matchup plan.
  • Know your mulligans.
  • Know your ideal curve.
  • Know your expected lethal patterns.
  • Use the same deck throughout the event unless you have a clear reason to switch.

He sees very little value in changing leaders between different portions of an event if you are less practiced with the alternate deck.

Comfort, familiarity, and matchup knowledge matter more than chasing a theoretical “perfect” list at the last minute.

Mihawk vs Rocks

Matchup Assessment

Impact views Mihawk versus Rocks as very favorable, but warns that players must understand why.

Mihawk’s major advantages:

  • Mihawk is a 6k leader.
  • Rocks must often commit DON!! to make its leader attacks meaningful.
  • If Rocks spends DON!! attacking, it can fall off curve.
  • Mihawk can remove or freeze the characters that make Rocks’ board function.
  • Mihawk can use Shanks, Vander Decken, Yasopp, Perona, Oden, and stuns to control board.
  • Rocks struggles if Newgate and Shiki cannot remain in play.
  • Once Mihawk wins board, Rocks’ non-counter cards become much worse.

Do Not Pure Race

Impact identifies two possible Mihawk plans:

Face pressure plus Luna

Mihawk can attack life, push Rocks low, then Luna the board.

The danger is Captain John plus Usopp spell.

Captain John plus Usopp spell can function like Rocks “Luna-ing Mihawk back”:

  • Rocks gets an extra turn.
  • Mihawk cannot efficiently kill the board.
  • Rocks can repeat pressure.
  • The matchup can become dangerous if Rocks has more Captain John and Usopp spell access than Mihawk has answers.

Board control first

Impact prefers board control.

The plan:

  1. Remove Newgate, Shiki, Linlin, and other Rocks engines.
  2. Use Yasopp plus Vander Decken to kill Shiki.
  3. Use Shanks as board control, not merely face damage.
  4. Use Oden to stop Captain John from resting again.
  5. Prevent Captain John plus Usopp spell from generating repeated turns.
  6. Once Rocks has no engine board, pressure life and finish quickly.

The main point is that Rocks cannot win without a board. It has cards in hand, but its hand only becomes defense, pressure, and value when Newgate, Shiki, and similar characters are in play.

Captain John Rule

When Rocks uses Captain John plus Usopp spell:

  • Do not panic and race blindly.
  • Get cards out of hand where possible.
  • Use Oden to freeze Captain John or the body that enables the next pressure turn.
  • Ensure Rocks cannot reuse the same Captain John for a second major turn.
  • Remove its other attackers so Captain John does not become a real threat.

Impact compares this to older Kid matchups. Keeping one protected unit alive is not enough if every other attacker is gone.

Mihawk vs Sabo

Matchup Assessment

Impact now strongly prefers the rest spell build into Sabo.

The Mihawk plan is:

  1. Starve Sabo early.
  2. Use the rest spell to remove its first 2-cost body.
  3. Use Yasopp to remove the next body.
  4. Use the rest spell or other interaction to remove Saul.
  5. Keep Sabo from developing a Giant-enabled board.
  6. Establish your own board advantage.
  7. Switch to life pressure only after you have won board.

This is a much different plan than blindly racing.

Why Starving Works

Sabo needs life, board, and card access to create a critical mass of 2-cost bodies plus Giant effects.

If Mihawk starves Sabo while removing early bodies:

  • Sabo cannot establish its normal pressure board.
  • Sabo cannot comfortably play its Luffy turns.
  • Saul or Loki becomes less impactful.
  • Its board lacks enough attackers to race.
  • Mihawk can develop while Sabo is forced to keep replacing pieces.

The goal is not to starve forever. The goal is to starve until Mihawk has a board lead. Once that happens, Mihawk transitions into a two-turn kill.

When to Change to Face

Impact’s practical trigger is when Sabo reaches a double-6-cost development or starts playing bodies that survive the early-control plan.

At that point:

  • Mihawk likely has a board advantage.
  • One of Mihawk’s bodies probably survived.
  • Mihawk can start chaining Oden into Sabo’s larger threats.
  • Mihawk can begin attacking life while still freezing or controlling board.
  • Sabo’s hand should already be limited from replacing early bodies.

The rest spell is strongest before Sabo’s board becomes active. Once Sabo has a larger board, Mihawk must use its usual Odin, Yasopp, Perona, Law, and Shanks tools to finish the game.

Mihawk vs Robin

Matchup Assessment

Impact sees Robin as slightly favored, approximately 55-45, but believes the matchup is playable if Mihawk approaches it as a gradual board-control game instead of a pure race.

Robin’s strengths:

  • Banish leader pressure.
  • Ramp.
  • Teach.
  • Sweet Generals.
  • Big Mom.
  • Giant.
  • Borsalino.
  • Large hand and healing late game.

Mihawk’s plan is to prevent Robin from having a board when its Big Mom turns begin.

EarlyEarly Game

Mihawk should:

  • Use Yasopp heavily.
  • Remove Robin’s early 4k bodies.
  • Keep Robin’s board narrow.
  • Avoid turning your own bodies into Sweet Generals trades when Robin has board.
  • Take the first one or two banish hits when needed to preserve hand for board defense.
  • Use smaller 5k attacks to pressure Robin’s low early hand.

Robin often spends several cards ramping:

  • Leader effect costs cards.
  • Ramp costs cards.
  • Pudding requires hand investment.
  • Teach, Big Mom, Giant, and heal lines require resources.

Mihawk should punish this with multiple small attacks rather than one big attack Robin can take and heal.

When to Attack Life

Attack life when:

  • Robin’s hand is low.
  • Robin has ramped and is short on counter.
  • Mihawk has already controlled the board.
  • Robin cannot easily play a Big Mom plus Giant turn.
  • Multiple 5k attacks force multiple small counters.
  • Robin is approaching one or zero life before a healing turn.

Impact prefers several 5k swings with smaller bodies, such as Perona and Kin’emon, over one giant leader swing when Robin is low on cards. Multiple attacks force Robin to make more decisions and may prevent it from comfortably healing back.

Shanks as Board Control

Against Robin, Shanks should often be used as a board-control tool.

Strong Shanks lines include:

  • Rest Robin’s board.
  • Use Mihawk bodies to attack smaller rested characters.
  • Vander Decken Big Mom.
  • Attack Teach with Shanks.
  • Force Giant while Robin also loses two or three cards.
  • Leave Robin with a slow next turn and insufficient hand to defend.

Mihawk does not need to kill Big Mom immediately in every game. It needs to make Robin’s Big Mom turn weak.

If Robin’s Big Mom is only a 10-DON!! heal and draw with no active attackers behind it, Mihawk is in a much better position.

Law Is Excellent

Impact considers Law very strong against Robin.

Law can:

  • Recycle Vander Decken.
  • Recycle Perona.
  • Create a blocker.
  • Attack when Robin has no efficient body to contest it.
  • Create repeated board-control turns.
  • Help Mihawk maintain wide development.
  • Help prevent Robin from establishing a second full Big Mom board.

Mihawk vs Kaido

Matchup Assessment

Impact considers Kaido a very favorable Mihawk matchup.

Mihawk should usually take first.

Why:

  • Kaido wants time and curve space.
  • Kaido wants the easy King ramp line.
  • Taking first interferes with Kaido’s preferred ramp progression.
  • Mihawk gets an extra attacker into play.
  • Mihawk can begin life pressure before Kaido reaches repeated boss turns.

Key Plan

  1. Develop Yasopp early.
  2. Use Yasopp to avoid Kaido leader interaction because it stands again.
  3. Pressure Kaido’s life early.
  4. Use Oden to stun King.
  5. Force Kaido to use leader effect and counter before Shanks.
  6. Use Shanks plus Vander Decken to answer Kaido’s major bodies.
  7. Continue pressing because Kaido can heal, so plan for one extra turn.

Attack Sizing

Against Kaido, attack sizes can manipulate leader effect.

  • Use 6k attacks to bait Kaido leader effect.
  • Use 7k attacks when you want Kaido to spend leader effect plus a card.
  • If Kaido spends leader effect on a smaller attack, it becomes harder for it to protect a major body later.
  • Once leader effect is spent, Shanks can threaten a large character with 17k or more.

The goal is to overload Kaido’s DON!!. Kaido may need to:

  • Develop.
  • Heal.
  • Use leader effect.
  • Defend life.
  • Protect a big body.
  • Answer Mihawk’s board.

It cannot always do all of those things in one turn.

Mihawk Build Choice

Rest Spell Build

Impact is leaning toward the rest spell build for major events.

The rest spell build is better into:

  • Enel.
  • Sabo.
  • Robin.
  • Early aggressive decks.
  • Random ladder or tournament decks.
  • Fields where board control is more valuable than mirror-only optimization.

The rest spell lets Mihawk:

  • Rest early characters.
  • Use free DON!! to remove them.
  • Deny early development.
  • Keep opponents off key curves.
  • Board control before they build a functional attack plan.
  • Survive Shanks pressure through flexible event defense.

Impact sees it as more than a single tech card. It is a full game plan.

If you include it, commit to the strategy:

  • Use it repeatedly.
  • Build board so you can attack the rested character.
  • Do not spend the spell as generic removal while behind.
  • Use it to establish a lead, then attack with five 7k swings once you have enough board.

Perona / Midrange Build

Impact also likes the Perona-heavy midrange version.

It is better when:

  • You expect many Mihawk mirrors.
  • You want stronger 7k body density.
  • You want more resilient board development.
  • You want Perona’s protection against rest-based board control.
  • You want more direct pressure through 8k leader swings and Shanks turns.

This build is more mirror-focused, while the rest spell build is more broad-field focused.

Hybrid Build

Impact sees hybrid builds as viable.

A likely hybrid direction:

  • 3 Law.
  • 2 Mihawk.
  • 3 Yasopp.
  • 3 Perona.
  • 3 Vander Decken.
  • Shanks plus Law/Bepo split.
  • A mix of Rad Beam, Stronger, DMG, and rest spells based on field expectations.

He considers the 3 Law, 2 Mihawk configuration preferable to a 4 Law, 1 Mihawk structure if rest spells are common. More Mihawks reduce dependence on one-drops, which can be removed by early rest-spell lines.

Event Choices

Stronger vs Luna

Impact prefers Stronger over Luna in rest-spell builds.

Why:

  • Stronger is a more flexible defensive event.
  • With four stages, the deck can often cast a spell cheaply or effectively for free.
  • Stronger can stop several 7k attacks.
  • Luna can be dead outside certain matchups.
  • Rest spell plus board control can reduce the number of bodies that need to be frozen.
  • In many Sabo and Luffy Ace situations, freezing two important attackers is enough.

Luna is still strong in dedicated mirror or Shanks-focused lists. But Impact sees Stronger as the more flexible choice for an all-field rest-spell build.

Dead Man’s Game

Impact rates Dead Man’s Game highly.

It is useful because it:

  • Taps Robin’s Giant DON!!.
  • Taps open DON!! in a Shanks plus Decken/Luna turn where opponent has one DON!! up.
  • Helps force lethal against Robin.
  • Can deny a key event line.
  • Is valuable in mirrors where the opponent needs one DON!! for defense or development.
  • Helps push through a blocker plus open DON!! scenario.

Impact considers DMG especially strong when Mihawk has already won board. Tapping the final defensive DON!! or Giant resource can make the opponent’s entire defensive turn collapse.

Four Shanks vs Three Shanks Plus Law/Bepo

Impact prefers four Shanks.

His reasoning:

  • Shanks is the deck’s strongest win condition.
  • Seeing Shanks plus Shanks is better than seeing Shanks plus Law/Bepo in most matchups.
  • The deck can use Shanks to create immediate damage, board control, and lethal pressure.
  • Law/Bepo is useful as a turn extension, but may be less important than a fourth copy of the main finisher.
  • The more aggressive Mihawk build especially wants four Shanks.

Law/Bepo remains playable as a one-of because it can:

  • Make Mihawk an 8k leader.
  • Pseudo-blank opposing 6k bodies.
  • Freeze a large attacker.
  • Buy an extra turn.
  • Create a 10k to 11k leader swing.
  • Bridge into the second Shanks turn.

But Impact is not convinced the 3 Shanks, 1 Law/Bepo split is stronger than simply playing four Shanks.

Mihawk vs Enel

Updated View: Starve and Control

Impact has changed his opinion on Mihawk versus Enel.

He now believes Mihawk should often starve Enel, control board, and make Enel run out of removal.

The plan:

  1. Do not feed Enel easy life cards.
  2. Use rest spell, Yasopp, Perona, Oden, and Vander Decken to control Enel’s board.
  3. Force Enel to spend removal spells.
  4. Do not give Enel more cards while it is trying to clear your board.
  5. Eventually stick one or two meaningful bodies.
  6. Play Shanks and remove Enel’s big threat.
  7. Force Enel to take life in one large board turn.
  8. Kill over the next one or two turns.

Why the plan works:

  • Enel’s removal spells do not replace themselves.
  • If Mihawk starves Enel, Enel must find both removal and a character repeatedly.
  • Enel may be forced to spend hand to remove Mihawk’s board without receiving new life cards.
  • Once Mihawk sticks multiple bodies, Enel cannot efficiently clear all of them.

Mihawk may go low in life while starving Enel, but that is acceptable if Enel is also running out of cards and cannot maintain its board.

Playing From Behind

Hand Context Determines the Plan

Impact explains that Mihawk should not play every game the same way.

When behind or when the opener is weak, determine the plan from the hand.

Shanks-heavy hand

If the hand contains multiple Shanks but lacks a strong early engine:

  • Play aggressively.
  • Send damage face.
  • Aim for a Shanks-based race.
  • Take calculated risks.
  • Force the opponent to have the specific answers.
  • Accept that the plan loses to more things, because the standard resource game was already losing.

Midrange-heavy hand

If the hand contains multiple 5-costs, 6-costs, Yasopps, Peronas, or board-control pieces:

  • Play a board-control game.
  • Remove one of the opponent’s double-development bodies.
  • Reduce the opponent’s attack count.
  • Make their double summon less impactful.
  • Stabilize, then convert board advantage into life pressure.

No-searcher hand

If Mihawk misses searchers while the opponent finds many:

  • You are unlikely to win a pure card-quality battle.
  • Look for a line where the opponent misses a key answer.
  • Push more aggressively.
  • Identify your actual possible win condition.
  • Do not pretend you can beat every possible hand.
  • Make the play that beats the widest realistic range.

Impact’s principle: When you are behind, play toward the line that wins if the opponent does not have the exact answer. If they have every answer, you were not winning anyway.

Tournament Mental Game

Sleep

Impact emphasizes sleeping earlier than you think you need to.

His recommendation:

  • Go to bed about one hour before your actual intended sleep time.
  • Give your mind time to settle.
  • Do not expect to instantly sleep after a stressful practice session or travel day.
  • Write down stressful thoughts, tasks, and tournament concerns before bed.
  • Telling yourself “I will revisit this later” helps the mind stop looping on it.

Caffeine and Food

For long tournament days:

  • Do not caffeinate immediately after waking up.
  • Wait roughly an hour until you are naturally awake.
  • Take caffeine strategically.
  • Consider a second caffeine point around the middle of the day.
  • Carry water.
  • Stay hydrated.
  • Bring small snacks.
  • Maintain stable energy rather than relying on one huge meal or one early caffeine spike.

Impact treats hydration and food as tournament preparation, not an afterthought. Dehydration can hurt performance as much as poor sleep.

Handling Losses

When a bad beat, misplay, or frustrating game happens:

  1. Write down what happened.
  2. Note the turn or decision that bothered you.
  3. Promise yourself you will review it later.
  4. Return focus to the next round.

Do not let the brain replay a loss all day. Writing it down gives you permission to stop thinking about it until the event is over.

Playing Pace

A long event is a marathon.

Impact’s guidance:

  • Do not rush because you are excited, nervous, or behind.
  • Play at an appropriate pace.
  • Think through turns.
  • Avoid slow play, but do not donate games by making fast mistakes.
  • Work backward from the winning board state.
  • Use searches to take the cards that support that endgame.

Finals and National-Level Events

What Changes at Finals

Impact believes finals-level events are somewhat more serious than a normal regional, but not dramatically different in baseline player quality.

Expect:

  • Fewer completely unprepared opponents.
  • More players who can win locals.
  • More top meta decks.
  • Fewer random rogue decks.
  • More Mihawk, Robin, Enel, Luffy Ace, and other established good decks.

Do not assume every opponent is a top-cut regional-level expert. Players can qualify through store championships, so the field still includes a broad skill range.

The practical difference is that “round one random rogue deck” is less likely. Tournament preparation should prioritize high-play-rate decks and established Tier 1 choices.

Playing on Stream or at Top Tables

Impact’s mental approach is to treat spectators and stream conditions as white noise.

His rule: The only thing that matters is playing the current match as well as possible.

He attributes this partly to esports experience. He does not attempt to manage the audience. He narrows attention to:

  • Board state.
  • Hand.
  • Life.
  • DON!!.
  • Matchup plan.
  • Next-turn lethal and survival math.

Final Meta Conclusions

Impact’s current OP17 recommendations are:

  • MihawkTop overall choice, especially with the rest spell build.
  • RobinOne of the two decks slightly above the rest, highly strong and resilient.
  • EnelVery strong, particularly with multiple Enels, multiple Pinks, Laws, Frankies, and a balanced Gamma Knife / Gum-Gum Lightning package.
  • Red-Green Luffy AceStrong, fast, and capable of exploiting the field, especially in quick-match structures.
  • RocksExtremely strong into most decks, but too risky if Mihawk remains highly represented.
  • SaboLess attractive because the rest spell and Yasopp give Mihawk much better early control.
  • NamiNot recommended; too many top decks punish it.
  • BoaPlayable but too polarized, especially because Robin is a difficult matchup.

Impact’s event-level recommendation is to pick Mihawk, Robin, Enel, or Luffy Ace based on comfort and matchup knowledge. Do not abandon a practiced deck chasing a marginal theoretical edge. A player who understands their deck, curve, search targets, counter plan, and matchup strategy will gain more equity than a player who copies an untested list the night before.
