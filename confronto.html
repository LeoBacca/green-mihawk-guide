LawSopp vs HawkRona

Le due versioni del mazzo messe a confronto argomento per argomento — Law + Yasopp (Impact) contro Mihawk 6c + Perona 5c (samsansOP) — più la tavola decisionale finale.

Trascrizione fedele del documento LawSopp vs HawkRona.docx (fonte in fonti/, non versionata). Confronta le due versioni del Green Mihawk OP17 argomento per argomento:

  • Sam: HawkRona — 6c Mihawk + Perona
  • Impact: LawSopp — Law + Yasopp

Ogni riga: la posizione di Sam, la posizione di Impact, e la colonna "What it means in practice".

TopicSam · HawkRonaImpact · LawSoppWhat it means in practice
Primary deck identityAggressive midrange tuned for consistency, clean resource conversion, and mirror performanceAggressive midrange tuned for board development, tempo, and rapid Shanks finishingBoth are proactive decks. Neither teacher believes OP17 Mihawk should primarily play as a slow control deck.
Central philosophyReduce failed hands and reach the Shanks pivot with the right cards, board state, life total, and counter resourcesBuild a board quickly, use extra DON to attack efficiently, and force the opponent into Shanks kill rangeSam favors a higher floor; Impact emphasizes a higher tempo ceiling.
Preferred 5/6-cost package6-cost Mihawk and PeronaTrafalgar Law and Yasopp, with Perona searcher as a reusable engineThis is the main structural difference between the lists.
Why play 6c Mihawk?It works independently, filters a card, can replace bricks, and produces a more stable development turnNot the preferred centerpiece; Impact favors Law's ability to double-develop and recycle utility bodiesSam sees filtering as a major form of card advantage in a deck with several non-counter, high-cost cards.
Why play Law?Law can be strong but is viewed as dependent on a good opener and surviving small bodiesLaw is a core engine: pick up Perona/searchers, develop another major body, and keep chaining valueThe disagreement is whether Law's setup cost is a meaningful liability or an intended, manageable part of the deck's engine.
Why play Yasopp?Strong card, especially against Sabo, but not enough payoff by itself to justify Law over Mihawk in a mirror-focused fieldOne of the deck's most important cards: early board control, resilient attacker, Law target, and Shanks supportIf you value early board interaction highly, Impact's evaluation of Yasopp becomes much more compelling.
Yasopp's self-restandHelpful, but not viewed as decisive in the mirrorCentral to the build: Yasopp can be developed rested through Law yet restand, making the Law turn less vulnerableImpact's LawSopp construction specifically solves the "Law leaves a body sideways" problem by making Yasopp the preferred deployment.
Perona's role (1c/5c)Freeze, disruption, and a way to make meaningful turns when Shanks is unavailable or delayedExtremely important searcher that should often be bounced by Law to find continuation piecesBoth highly value Perona. Sam emphasizes her independent freeze/filter utility; Impact emphasizes repeated access through Law.
Perona + Law interactionRecognized as good card flow, but Sam believes it does not outweigh Mihawk's independent filtering and board qualityA signature line: bounce Perona with Law and play a 5c, replay her, and look for Shanks, Luna, events, or follow-up playsLawSopp gains ceiling and deeper digging when it can repeatedly convert Perona.
Brick management6c Mihawk is preferred because it can actively filter a brick out of handUse Samurai, Coffin Boat, Kinemon, leader-generated DON, and Law/Perona loops to avoid getting cloggedBoth acknowledge green Mihawk has bricks. Sam relies more on built-in filtering; Impact relies more on active card cycling and sequencing.
View of raw card drawDrawing an extra card is not always better if the original brick remains in your handHigh draw density lets the deck find otherwise unsearchable pieces and maintain follow-upSam differentiates "more cards" from "better cards"; Impact values the volume of looks generated by the deck's engines.
Opener dependenceLow-to-moderate: Mihawk still functions if the opponent clears early charactersHigher: Law is best when there is a worthwhile body to pick up and convertSam's version is more resilient when early searchers die or when the opening hand is uneven.
Small-body vulnerabilityLess reliant on small bodies remaining alive before the 5/6-cost turnNeeds early bodies to maximize Law, although the deck can develop many of them and convert them into valueAgainst decks that efficiently remove 1-costs, Sam's structural argument gets stronger.
Samurai interactionSamurai remains an important source of advantage; Sam dislikes that Law can make Samurai lines awkwardSamurai remains central, but Impact's guide emphasizes using excess DON to convert Samurai and avoid brickingSam sees potential anti-synergy: Law wants a body to bounce, while Samurai can expose/remove the bodies Law needs.
Early-game objectiveEstablish draw/search, preserve resources, and prepare the correct curve into the mirror/Shanks pivotDraw cards, search for curve pieces, take early life when appropriate, and set up future developmentBoth prioritize card access early; Impact is more explicit about taking life to acquire combo pieces.
Midgame objectiveDevelop enough pressure while preserving the correct hand, counter thresholds, and board state for ShanksEstablish 6K attackers, convert them into 7K swings with free DON, and pressure life every turnSam is more cautious about resource structure; Impact is more explicit about relentless tempo conversion.
Use of extra DONSpend it where it advances life mapping or allows important defensive/event management; avoid wasting it on low-value developmentUse it aggressively to push damage, play searchers/Samurai, cycle bricks, and maintain board pressureBoth recognize free DON as Mihawk's defining resource advantage. Impact pushes harder toward immediate damage.
Attacking philosophyIn the mirror, attack sizing and life preservation must be designed around the Shanks raceAttack aggressively, especially with 7K lines, to force the opponent into Shanks kill rangeSam's attacks are more mirror-clock-focused; Impact's attacks are more universally pressure-focused.
Preferred board stateStable, active bodies and a hand that can execute a Shanks turn without collapsing to counter pressureWide board of sticky 6K bodies that can repeatedly swing for 7K and force life/cardsSam prefers robustness; Impact wants density of attackers.
Board protectionImportant, but Sam's analysis values whether the board actually survives the opponent's Shanks/Luna pivotA major priority: midgame 6K bodies are how the deck converts wins, and Billionfold should often protect themImpact places particular weight on preserving 6K attackers because they become the bridge to Shanks lethal.
Shanks evaluationThe defining bomb of the mirror: the 12K rush swing changes life thresholds and forces careful countering"Does everything": finisher, removal, setup for removal, setup for Luna, and a fast closing mechanismBoth see Shanks as the reason OP17 Mihawk's endgame is dramatically stronger than earlier versions.
Shanks timingDo not delay the relevant Shanks turn in the mirror; if you wait, the opponent can resolve theirs firstDeploy Shanks in the late game and often aim to finish by the second 10-DON turnBoth want timely Shanks; Sam stresses race math, while Impact stresses converting the midgame into a fast close.
Shanks + LunaA decisive mirror sequence because Shanks can make the opponent's board vulnerable to Luna/freeze effectsA primary tempo tool: freeze the opposing board while Shanks pushes damage and prepares lethalBoth see this pairing as powerful; Sam discusses it more as mirror interaction, Impact more as a general finishing pattern.
Shanks + Vander DeckenKey answer to high-cost bombs and an important reason Kaido has become less attractiveA major matchup tool for dealing with 7–8+ DON threats while maintaining your own pressureBoth treat Decken as a premium answer to large characters, not merely a generic removal card.
Law & BepoA compact "second bomb" that can replace a complicated multi-card defensive sequence and buy an extra turnUsually a one-of extension tool; useful when one extra turn is needed but no longer the deck's main identitySam gives Law & Bepo more strategic weight in the mirror; Impact treats it as a powerful but secondary extension card.
Role of freezesFreeze effects are central to interrupting an opposing Shanks turn or reducing a board's conversionFreezes are for tempo, not prolonged control; use them to buy the turn needed to killThis is mostly agreement. Neither wants to freeze merely to extend a game indefinitely.
View of long control gamesAvoid them when possible; the build is designed to win the critical bomb turns efficientlyExplicitly rejects OP14-style long Law & Bepo control loops as the deck's main planStrong agreement: OP17 Mihawk should finish, not endlessly stall.
Mirror priorityHighest deck-building priority; Sam expects Mihawk to be common enough that mirror equity should shape flex slotsImportant matchup, but Impact's guide focuses on executing the core pressure curve and damage mapSam is more willing to sacrifice some non-mirror edge for mirror tools and consistency.
Mirror going secondVery desirable because the second player can establish the more favorable double-development/Shanks sequenceAlso says to go second; follow the normal curve and use early draw to secure counter and bomb accessStrong agreement: going second is preferred in the mirror.
Mirror life managementYou often need to preserve at least two life before the opponent's Shanks turn because dropping to 0 is extremely dangerousCounter smaller swings early because Shanks attacks can become too large to defend efficientlySam gives more exact conceptual weight to life thresholds; Impact gives practical attack-size guidance.
Mirror attack thresholdsFocus is on putting the opponent to 0 first while retaining enough counter to survive retaliationRecommends 7K frequently, 8K when no DON is up, 9K when 8 rested cards are unavailable, and 10K to attack through BillionfoldImpact gives concrete breakpoints for the mirror.
Mirror tech: Stun BonneyMajor innovation in Sam's approach; can deny the opposing Shanks turn and reverse who gets the first bomb pivotNot a central element of Impact's published standard list or matchup planThis is a substantial practical distinction. Sam sees Bonney as a way to fix going-first mirror weakness.
View of 7c/9c Shanks linesLess attractive because Vander Decken interacts favorably and the newer freeze environment reduces their valueFocus remains on 10c Shanks as the major endgame bombSam is more explicit that Decken has narrowed the usefulness of prior Shanks options.
Sabo matchupAcknowledges Yasopp meaningfully improves the matchup, but does not believe that alone outweighs HawkRona's mirror advantagesIdentifies Sabo as one of the more difficult matchups and builds specifically to answer it through Yasopp, Oden, Decken, and careful counteringSabo is Impact's strongest practical argument for LawSopp.
Sabo planAttack proactively rather than attempting a full starvation strategy; selectively attack rested bodies when offeredUse Yasopp to clear small bodies, preserve Decken for Luffy/Loki, freeze high-impact units with Oden, and aim for a two-turn Shanks killSam is more skeptical of trying to completely control Sabo; Impact leans more into selective board control.
Sabo and YasoppYasopp's key benefit is turning an opponent's unit sideways so you can contest it while retaining your own active bodyYasopp is the early answer that prevents Sabo's low-cost value bodies from snowballingBoth agree Yasopp is good into Sabo; they differ on how much that should determine the full list.
Kaido matchupShanks + Vander Decken materially improves the matchup; Decken is a major reason Kaido is less appealing into MihawkAlso emphasizes Shanks + Decken as a huge tempo exchange; Yasopp can help restand Shanks and attack safelyBoth see Decken as critical. Sam leans toward consistent access; Impact emphasizes on-board execution.
Kaido attack sequencingPrioritize avoiding inefficient exchanges and maintaining the bomb-turn planAttack with 6K characters at 7K to play around leader reduction; use leader DON differently when setting up lethalImpact provides more specific tactical sequencing guidance.
Robin matchupStun Bonney and freeze-related options can incidentally improve yellow/mom board turnsYasopp clears 4K units; pressure early so Robin cannot freely establish two Big Mom turns; use Shanks + Decken/Luna to remove key bodiesImpact's guide gives a detailed Robin plan.
Rocks matchupMihawk is naturally strong; Law & Bepo can be an alternate method of invalidating their boardUse Luna heavily, account for Captain John stall, pressure with appropriate attack sizes, and respect ShikiBoth are optimistic about the matchup.
Enel matchupPreserve appropriate life and prepare Shanks turns; freezes can limit their board conversionUse low-cost bodies to clear 1-costs, push life with leader, reserve Oden for large Enels, and avoid losing more than one life per turnImpact's guide covers defense and combo timing against Enel. Defensive eventsBe highly intentional about leaving DON up, especially because full Shanks/Luna turns consume most available DONGenerally spend around one DON per turn for events when appropriate, but calculate actual incoming swings and weave events out before they clogBoth agree event management is a real skill test in the deck.
Coffin BoatSam is lower on high event/Boat density than older builds because newer bomb turns frequently consume DON and some events matter lessImpact explicitly includes and uses Coffin Boat as part of the deck's card-flow and brick-management infrastructureThis is a secondary but meaningful deck-construction difference. 2K counter philosophyStrongly values ordinary 2K counter density because many mirror attacks are one-card hitsEmphasizes managing the deck's brick count and using counter events/resources without becoming cloggedSam's version leans toward reliable middle-game defense; Impact focuses more on actively converting awkward cards.
Main source of advantageCard quality, clean sequencing, filtering, freeze timing, and mirror disruptionCard volume, repeated searches, double development, resilient Yasopp pressure, and free-DON attacksSam improves quality of resources; Impact increases quantity and tempo of resource conversion.
Main failure modePotentially gives up some direct board-control edge, especially into Sabo, by not fully exploiting YasoppCan draw the wrong mix of setup cards/bricks, lose Law targets, or face awkward Samurai/Law sequencingPick Sam's list if you hate inconsistency; pick Impact's list if you hate losing the board early.
Skill testLife mapping, counter thresholds, identifying when to skip lower-value development, and navigating the mirror bomb raceSequencing searches before Law, Law target management, board protection, identifying damage breakpoints, and planning multistep lethal turnsBoth are difficult decks, but their difficult decisions occur at different points in the game.
Best player profilePlayer who wants consistency, anticipates many Mihawk mirrors, and prefers a disciplined, calculated bomb-turn planPlayer who enjoys active board combat, technical sequencing, repeated Perona loops, and applying pressure every turnThe "better" list may be less about raw power than about your local field and decision-making strengths.
Best metagame profileMihawk-heavy, open, uncertain, or interaction-heavy field where clean execution is paramountSabo-heavy, small-body-heavy, board-centric field where Yasopp can repeatedly produce tempoSam's build hedges against expected mirrors; Impact's build attacks established boards.
Most transferable lessonDo not confuse drawing more cards with improving hand quality; life/counter math determines whether Shanks actually winsDo not play Mihawk passively; build the board, convert free DON into 7K pressure, and map the opponent into Shanks rangeCombining these lessons produces the strongest overall Mihawk fundamentals.
