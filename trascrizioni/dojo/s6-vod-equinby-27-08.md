# VOD REVIEW WITH EQUINBY

### OP17 and OP16.5 Gameplay, Matchup Theory, and Decision-Making Frameworks

### Session Overview

This session is a broad VOD review led by Elijah. Rather than staying on one deck, he reviews several games and uses them to teach a universal decision-making framework:

- Understand which deck benefits if the game becomes shorter or longer.
- Do not treat life, hand, board, and DON!! as separate resources. Every decision exchanges one of them for another.
- Attack sizing matters. A 5k, 6k, and 7k swing can create completely different outcomes depending on the opponent’s deck.
- Use matchup knowledge to identify which cards are real and which cards are effectively bricks.
- Do not spend resources defending bodies that cannot survive the entire opposing turn.
- Build your game plan around the opposing deck’s actual win condition, not the card that looks scariest in the moment.

## ELBAF SABO VS MIHAWK

### Matchup Identity

Elijah sees Sabo versus Mihawk as a matchup that should favor Sabo when the Sabo player understands the race.

The reason is structural:

- Sabo can put more bodies and more stats into play earlier.
- Mihawk gets stronger as the game progresses.
- Mihawk gains access to more DON!! for events, stuns, and bigger development.
- Mihawk eventually has access to Shanks, Decken, Luna, and massive turns that can invalidate Sabo’s board.
- Sabo does not want to give Mihawk the time required to establish that late-game control package.

The core conclusion:

> Sabo wants the game to be short. Mihawk wants the game to go long.

This is not just a matchup-specific lesson. Elijah uses it as a model for how players should analyze every matchup.

Before deciding whether to clear board, push life, or develop, ask:

1. Which deck is strongest in the early game?
2. Which deck is strongest in the late game?
3. Does my current line speed up the game or extend it?
4. Does extending the game benefit me or my opponent?

### Die Roll and Going Second

Elijah believes Sabo should generally choose second into Mihawk.

Going second matters because:

- Sabo gets the stronger curve.
- It develops more efficiently.
- It can establish Giant plus bodies faster.
- It has greater ability to present multiple attacks before Mihawk’s major control turns.
- Mihawk’s going-first pressure can be difficult, but Sabo’s second-player development helps compensate.

A Sabo player who accidentally chooses first gives up a meaningful structural edge.

### Early-Game Plan

Elijah’s preferred Sabo progression against Mihawk is not to rush out Saul on the first available turn.

If Sabo goes first, he prefers something closer to:

1. Play a 2-cost body on the 3-DON!! turn.
2. Swing 5k.
3. On 5 DON!!, develop two 2-cost bodies again.
4. Swing another 5k.
5. On 7 DON!!, establish Saul or Loki.
6. If Saul is used, pair it with another 2-cost body.
7. Enter the following turn with a board that can attack multiple times.

Why this is better than Saul on 5:

- Sabo wants to chip Mihawk early before Mihawk has large defensive options.
- Double 2-cost development creates more board density.
- Saul alone does not create enough immediate pressure.
- A delayed Saul can create a five-wide board and several 6k to 7k attacks.
- Mihawk will often stop only one or two attacks with Perona, Oden, or Yasopp, leaving Sabo with multiple remaining swings.

### Saul and Loki Roles

#### Saul

Saul is powerful, but fragile.

Elijah sees Saul as a card that:

- Enables the Giant package.
- Makes the board function.
- Can create a large conversion turn.
- Often must remain standing because losing it can collapse the deck’s entire structure.

Saul is not always the best immediate play. If Sabo has no second Giant or no follow-up body, playing Saul too early can create a bottleneck where Mihawk removes it and Sabo loses its entire plan.

#### Loki

Loki is more resilient than Saul.

Loki is often preferable when:

- The opponent has removal.
- Sabo needs a Giant that can safely attack.
- Saul is the only remaining Giant.
- Sabo needs to avoid being locked out of its leader or Giant-dependent turns.

Loki’s advantage is that it can often attack without immediately collapsing the board plan. Saul frequently has to remain active because it is too important to lose.

### Zoro as the Anti-Shanks Tool

A key Elijah lesson in this matchup is using Zoro to survive the Mihawk Shanks plus Decken turn.

Mihawk’s intended 10-DON!! turn often involves:

- Shanks plus Decken.
- Stuns or freezes.
- A major body that invalidates Sabo’s board.
- Pressure that attempts to remove Sabo’s Giant and lock its attacks.

Sabo’s answer is often Zoro.

The Sabo player should:

- Have a Zoro available before Mihawk’s 9-DON!! or 10-DON!! control turn.
- Preferably play the Zoro from the Luffy turn, so it has not been exposed earlier.
- Be willing to spend a huge amount of hand defending Zoro.
- Treat Zoro as the card that keeps the whole board functional through the critical turn.

Elijah’s core idea is that the Sabo player may need to “dump the hand” to protect Zoro.

Why that can be correct:

- Mihawk can attack with several 6k bodies, then a 12k Shanks or similar large attacker.
- Sabo may need to give multiple 1ks and 2ks.
- But if Zoro survives, Mihawk should die on the following turn because Sabo has already landed several earlier attacks.
- Mihawk cannot cleanly Decken Sabo if Sabo’s Zoro and board remain functional.

### Double Giant Line

Elijah describes a stronger Sabo sequence when the player finds two Giants:

1. Giant on 5.
2. Giant on 7.
3. Luffy on 9.
4. Use Luffy to deploy Zoro.
5. Mihawk attacks the Zoro with multiple 6k swings.
6. Sabo counters the 6ks.
7. Mihawk sends a large 12k attack.
8. Sabo can allow Zoro to die to the 12k.
9. Mihawk uses Decken on the second Giant.
10. Sabo still retains the first Giant and Luffy, leaving a functioning board.

This line is stronger because it forces Mihawk to spend its control tools on multiple different threats. Sabo does not need every body to live. It needs enough of the board to remain active after Mihawk spends its big turn.

### Why the Reviewed Game Was Lost

Elijah identifies both draw issues and gameplay mistakes.

The Sabo player:

- Missed early pressure.
- Did not swing 5k on the 3-DON!! turn.
- Did not double-develop 2-cost bodies on the 5-DON!! turn.
- Played Saul in a spot where it did not advance the actual race.
- Fell behind on board density.
- Allowed the game to progress into Mihawk’s ideal late-game window.

By the time the player used Gravity Blade, clearing Mihawk’s board may have delayed defeat, but it did not create a realistic winning route.

This is an important distinction: A play can prevent you from losing immediately without helping you win.

Gravity Blading Mihawk in that spot gave Sabo more time, but Sabo was already too far behind to use that time productively.

## ROBIN VS ROCKS

### Matchup Identity

Elijah sees Robin versus Rocks as a difficult matchup for Robin, but more playable than Sabo versus Rocks.

Robin’s general plan is unusual:

- Starve Rocks except for leader swings.
- Heal through Rocks’ pressure.
- Wait for Rocks to turn meaningful bodies sideways.
- Use Sweet Generals and board interaction to clear those rested bodies.
- Slowly exhaust Rocks’ hand and board despite the leader drawing cards.

At face value, starving Rocks seems strange because Rocks draws two cards every turn. However, Robin’s plan is not to win instantly through starvation. The plan is to make Rocks develop without gaining life resources, then punish the board once it turns.

### Going Second

Elijah strongly supports Robin going second into Rocks.

Reasons:

- Robin gets more DON!! for repeated searches.
- Robin can more reliably find Pudding, 3-cost plays, and 10-cost Big Mom.
- Robin gets better access to its ramp and healing progression.
- The 6-DON!! turn becomes more efficient because Robin can use a Big Mom spell rather than spending leader skill repeatedly.
- Robin has more room to maintain hand quality while preparing for Rocks’ early Linlin turns.

### Search Sequencing

Elijah highlights a major fundamental rule: Resolve the smallest search first, then resolve the larger search.

Why:

- The small search provides information.
- The larger search can then use that information.
- You always want maximum information before committing to the higher-impact search.
- You may learn that you no longer need to search, or that one card should now be prioritized over another.

For example:

- Look at 3 cards first.
- Then use the search that sees 5 cards.
- Do not automatically reverse the order.

The only exception is when the player is uncertain whether they will use every search effect. If you know you will use all of them, start with the smallest one.

### Pudding and Big Mom Spell Management

Elijah likes preserving the card that allows Robin to double-ramp efficiently on the 6-DON!! turn.

The logic:

- Rocks often plays Linlin on 5.
- If Rocks develops the expected Big Mom card, Robin can use its Big Mom spell on 6.
- This can avoid spending leader effect twice.
- Robin saves a card in hand compared with a leader-effect-heavy sequence.
- Robin maintains a smoother route to Sweet Generals and 10-cost Big Mom.

A player should not discard this setup piece too casually just because another card looks playable now.

### When to Use Smoothie

Elijah is skeptical of Smoothie in many Rocks positions.

Smoothie is best when:

- The opponent is countering banish attacks.
- The opponent has a small hand.
- The player needs to force hand pressure immediately.
- The game is a yellow mirror where life cards and trigger access matter heavily.

Smoothie is weaker when:

- Rocks is willing to take banish hits.
- Robin cannot meaningfully punish Rocks’ hand.
- Playing Smoothie delays Big Mom development.
- Robin has better healing and board-control lines available.

### Robin’s Starve Plan

The proposed Robin plan against Rocks is:

1. Attack primarily with leader.
2. Do not feed Rocks unnecessary life resources.
3. Heal regularly.
4. Develop Sweet Generals and Big Mom bodies.
5. Wait for Rocks to turn Newgate, Shiki, Linlin, or other important characters.
6. Clear the rested board.
7. Keep Rocks low enough that it cannot rebuild efficiently.

The theory is that Rocks eventually runs low on cards because:

- It must counter.
- It must develop board pieces.
- It must spend hand cards through leader effects.
- Several Rocks bodies do not replace themselves as efficiently as they appear.
- Robin’s repeated heals reduce Rocks’ ability to end the game.

Elijah acknowledges this plan is not fully solved. Rocks can simply develop more cards, keep major bodies standing, and wait until it has multiple 10-cost developments plus Shikis and Newgate. This is why Robin versus Rocks remains a matchup requiring more testing.

### Big Mom Timing

Elijah spots a missed opportunity to play 10-cost Big Mom earlier.

The key lesson: If you have enough life, enough hand, and the opponent has limited ways to punish the board, play the major threat before it becomes too late.

In the reviewed line:

- Robin had multiple important bodies.
- Rocks had limited immediate pressure.
- Robin had enough counter and a heal available.
- Playing 10-cost Big Mom would have created a much larger board and made future Sweet Generals turns stronger.

Instead, delaying the Big Mom gave Rocks more room to build its own board.

### Borsalino and Deck Construction

A Robin player in the session describes playing Borsalino as a key survival piece.

Borsalino matters because:

- It creates a meaningful body.
- It can stabilize against multiple attacks.
- It can help protect the board.
- It pairs with Pudding and Giant lines.
- It gives Robin survival turns that other cards cannot provide.

Elijah agrees that Borsalino is extremely strong, but notes that Robin list space is tight. The deck has many attractive options:

- Teach
- More searchers
- Borsalino
- “You’ll Frighten Me!”
- More Big Mom pieces
- Additional trigger density
- More healing cards

The key deckbuilding question is not whether a card is good. It is whether it is better than the card it replaces.

## MIHAWK VS PENEL

### Matchup Identity

The Mihawk versus Enel review focuses on life management, attack sizing, one-drop management, and how to avoid giving Enel high-value Mamaragan turns.

Mihawk’s overall plan is:

- Take early life liberally.
- Develop Mihawk plus Oden.
- Preserve enough board to create wide future turns.
- Use Perona, Oden, and Law/Bepo to control Enel’s relevant attackers.
- Avoid giving Enel efficient Mamaragan targets.
- Convert the board into large life pressure once Enel can no longer afford to use its events freely.

### Choosing What to Counter Early

Elijah gives a highly transferable counter framework.

When deciding whether to take an early 6k attack, ask:

1. How many future attacks bigger than 6k will occur before my 10-DON!! turn?
2. Can those future attacks be stopped with Oden, Perona, events, or board interaction?
3. If the future attacks are 6k or smaller, would I simply spend the same counter card later anyway?
4. What can I draw from life that improves my next turn?

His conclusion is that Mihawk should generally be willing to take the first two attacks, including a 6k attack, unless there is a specific hand structure or lethal concern.

The logic:

- Mihawk needs several future attacks larger than 6k to make taking the current 6 actually punishing.
- Mihawk often has events and stuns that prevent future attacks.
- Life can reveal a playable Perona, Kin’emon, stage, or event.
- A counter card spent now may be the same card spent later against a smaller attack anyway.

### When to Respect Hand-Rip Law

Elijah does not ignore hand-rip Law completely.

Counter early to avoid Law only when:

- Losing specific cards immediately loses the game.
- Your hand contains one crucial Oden and one crucial 5-cost.
- The opponent’s Law can remove both of the only cards that make your curve function.
- Your hand has no redundancy.

Do not overplay around Law when your hand has redundant pieces.

If the opponent can rip one Oden and one Mihawk but you still have functional curve and board development, that is annoying but not necessarily worth sacrificing multiple life cards or counters early.

### Mamaragan Attack Sizing

A major correction in the Enel review is not leaving DON!! unused while attacking for only 5k.

If Mihawk is not using its remaining DON!! for:

- Samurai
- Boat
- an event
- a needed defensive line
- another development action

then it should increase the attack size.

Why:

- Enel can Mamaragan a 5k attack.
- Mamaragan preserves Enel’s hand while adding life.
- That extra life later absorbs a 7k attack.
- The result can be a huge effective advantage for Enel.

A 5k attack into a ready Mamaragan may be worse than a 6k or 7k attack that forces real resources.

The rule:

> Do not give Enel a clean Mamaragan if you could have attacked for a real number instead.

### One-Drop Management

Elijah sees triple Ohm as a board that must be respected.

If Enel has multiple Ohms:

- Mihawk’s smaller bodies may die regardless of whether they remain active.
- The board can trade into Mihawk’s medium attackers.
- Samurai can become correct even with larger bodies if those bodies are doomed anyway.
- Drawing cards from Samurai and Mihawk cycling may be better than preserving bodies that cannot live.

Normally, Elijah would not Samurai major bodies in the matchup. But against triple Ohm, the board can be so hostile that extracting the draw immediately becomes reasonable.

The concept is: If a body is certainly dying, convert it into value before it dies.

### Mihawk Plus Oden

The standard development is Mihawk plus Oden.

The goals are:

- Establish more than one relevant body.
- Freeze an attacker.
- Reduce Enel’s available attack count.
- Make Enel spend attacks on board instead of life.
- Create a board that can eventually attack for multiple 7k swings.

The Enel player can remove one body. The Mihawk player wins by forcing Enel to answer several bodies over multiple turns.

## MIHAWK VS ACE LUFFY

### Matchup Identity

Elijah considers Ace Luffy a low-play-rate deck that is deceptively easy to beat once the opponent understands its list construction.

Its major weakness is extreme lack of counter.

The deck effectively plays:

- Very few 2ks.
- Almost no meaningful 1ks.
- Low-cost cards that are usually placed on board rather than held.
- Bricks and event-heavy hands.

Elijah’s simplified matchup rule is: Attack Ace Luffy with repeated 7k swings and it dies.

He states that the deck can fold to one 6k attack followed by several 7k attacks because it cannot meaningfully protect life through normal counter.

### Key Intelligence: Count the Counter

Against Ace Luffy:

- Do not assume it has normal counter density.
- Do not play as though you need giant 10k to 12k attacks.
- 7k, 7k attack patterns are often ideal.
- Repeated moderate attacks are better than inefficient overcommitted swings.
- The opponent’s real 1k cards are often cards they want to play, not cards they want to hold.

This is a tournament-level scouting lesson: If you know a deck’s counter distribution, you know which attack sizes actually matter.

### Mihawk Plan

Mihawk should:

- Attack face.
- Use Yasopp aggressively.
- Let Yasopp restand itself through its effect when possible.
- Stop wasting turns on board control.
- Force Ace Luffy to defend life with cards it does not have.

Elijah critiques a line where Mihawk skipped two face swings.

The issue was severe because Ace Luffy does not have the counter density to survive. Every missed 6k or 7k attack is a missed opportunity to exploit the deck’s main structural weakness.

### Why Board Control Is Usually Wrong

Mihawk cannot reasonably starve Ace Luffy and then clear its board like it might against a smaller-body deck.

Reasons:

- Ace Luffy’s relevant bodies are too large.
- Mihawk’s attacks are better spent at face.
- Ace Luffy is not built to defend repeated life pressure.
- A board-control plan extends the game, which helps the deck find more development and creates unnecessary variance.

If Mihawk has unused DON!! and is not spending it on Boat, Samurai, development, or events, it should attach the DON!! and make a larger face attack.

## LIFE, COUNTER, AND RESOURCE THEORY

### The Core Counter Question

Elijah spends substantial time explaining why counter decisions should be based on the entire future turn cycle, not the immediate attack.

The wrong question is: “Can I counter this attack?”

The correct question is: “If I do not counter this attack, what future attacks become impossible to survive, and what do I gain by taking the life now?”

### When to Take Early Life

Taking early life is usually correct when:

- The attack is 5k or 6k.
- Your future defense can stop larger attacks.
- You have Oden, Perona, events, or stuns that reduce future attack count.
- The life card could be a playable card on your next turn.
- You are not immediately entering lethal range.
- Your hand has important but awkward cards and you need more options.
- The counter spent now would likely be spent later against a similarly sized attack.

### When to Counter Early

Counter early when:

- The opponent has enough attacks to deal two or more life in the same turn.
- Your next turn requires a specific hand size or life total.
- You have a crucial card that needs protection from hand-rip.
- Taking life creates a known lethal threshold.
- You need to maintain a specific number of life to survive a later double-attack or rush sequence.
- The card spent is less valuable than the future life it saves.

### Counter as Future Value

Elijah explains card value through future exchanges.

Example:

- Countering a 5k now with Franky may save one life.
- If you instead take the 5k, you may later need three cards to survive a 7k or 8k sequence.
- In that case, spending Franky early may be correct.
- But if taking the 5k gives you a playable card and future attacks remain manageable, taking is better.

The player must compare:

- One card now.
- Two or three cards later.
- The chance the life card improves the curve.
- The board state created by either decision.

### Do Not Hold a Card You Cannot Play

A recurring example involves Franky.

If countering with Franky means you still have a workable Pink turn or another playable line, that can be correct.

But do not counter with Franky and then pass with no development if playing Franky was your only meaningful turn. That is a disaster because the card was not merely a counter. It was your entire board development.

The rule: Do not spend your only playable card as counter unless you know your remaining hand still has a functional turn.

## GENERAL MATCHUP FRAMEWORK

Elijah’s most transferable teaching segment is his framework for determining whether each deck wants the game to be long or short.

### Step One: Compare Early Boards

Ask:

- Which deck develops more stats early?
- Which deck gets more bodies out earlier?
- Which deck gets the first major pressure turn?
- Which deck has the better early attacks?

If your deck has the early stat advantage, you usually want to shorten the game.

### Step Two: Compare Late-Game Tools

Ask:

- Which deck gains better events with more DON!!?
- Which deck has stronger 10-DON!! turns?
- Which deck has inevitable boss monsters?
- Which deck gets more value from long games?
- Which deck eventually invalidates the other deck’s board?

If the opponent’s late-game package is stronger, you need to pressure before it arrives.

### Step Three: Identify the Opponent’s Actual Win Condition

Do not focus only on the visible threat.

Examples:

- Mihawk’s win condition against Sabo is not simply “play Oden.” It is surviving long enough to use Shanks, Decken, Luna, and major stun turns.
- Enel’s win condition against Mihawk is not one 6k attack. It is establishing enough one-drops and removal that Mihawk cannot retain a board.
- Robin’s win condition against Rocks is not merely healing. It is reaching a board where Rocks must turn meaningful characters into Sweet Generals removal.
- Ace Luffy’s win condition is not countering efficiently. It is winning before opponents recognize that it has almost no counter.

### Step Four: Make Every Turn Serve That Plan

Once you know whether the game should be fast or slow:

- Do not take lines that only delay losing.
- Do not clear board if it does not help you win.
- Do not play a late-game value line if your deck loses long games.
- Do not race if the opponent cannot beat your endgame.
- Do not overprotect a body that does not enable the next winning turn.

## PRACTICAL STUDY GUIDE

### Sabo vs Mihawk

- Choose second when possible.
- Do not rush Saul onto the 5-DON!! turn without follow-up.
- Build board with 2-costs first.
- Chip Mihawk with early 5k swings.
- Establish Giant on 7, then create a wide attack turn.
- Save Zoro to survive Shanks plus Decken.
- Be willing to spend large amounts of hand protecting Zoro.
- End the game before Mihawk reaches full Shanks, Decken, Luna control.

### Robin vs Rocks

- Choose second.
- Sequence small searches before large searches.
- Preserve cards that enable efficient 6-DON!! ramp.
- Do not assume Smoothie is always the best play.
- Heal and build board while Rocks develops.
- Avoid feeding Rocks unnecessary life.
- Punish rested Rocks engine bodies.
- Play 10-cost Big Mom early enough to matter.
- Track whether Rocks can simply develop through your starvation plan.

### Mihawk vs Enel

- Take early 5k and 6k attacks liberally.
- Respect hand-rip Law only when your hand has no redundancy.
- Do not give Enel free Mamaragan on 5k if unused DON!! can create a larger swing.
- Develop Mihawk plus Oden.
- Respect multiple Ohms.
- Samurai doomed bodies rather than letting them die without value.
- Build wide enough that Enel cannot remove every relevant character.
- Keep enough counter for the future lethal turn, not every small attack.

### Mihawk vs Ace Luffy

- Attack face.
- Use repeated 7k swings.
- Do not waste turns clearing board.
- Do not assume the deck has normal counter density.
- Yasopp is especially valuable because it can create a free rest and restand.
- Every missed face attack gives Ace Luffy unnecessary time.
- Moderate repeated attacks are better than one overcommitted giant attack.

### Robin vs Ace Luffy

- Treat the matchup as highly unfavorable until more testing proves otherwise.
- Do not expect normal banish and heal control to function automatically.
- Be aware that low-play-rate decks can still be severe counterpicks.
- Do not overprepare for this matchup at the cost of core meta matchups unless Ace Luffy is common locally.

## FINAL TAKEAWAYS

Elijah’s session is fundamentally about playing the matchup, not playing the card.

The core lessons are:

- **Determine who wants the game to be fast and who wants it to be long.**
- **Do not defend a body that cannot survive the entire turn.**
- **Use attack sizing to deny the opponent’s best defensive exchange.**
- **Take early life when future attacks are manageable and life improves your next turn.**
- **Counter early only when the future math proves that the life matters more than the card.**
- **Know the opponent’s counter density before choosing attack numbers.**
- **Build a board that forces the opponent to spend whole turns on interaction.**
- **Do not mistake a move that delays defeat for a move that creates a win condition.**
- **Prioritize testing toward popular, hard, and high-stakes matchups instead of spending most of your time on low-play-rate rogues.**

A tournament-ready player should be able to explain, before their first attack each turn, whether their objective is to race, stabilize, starve, protect a key body, or force lethal. When that answer is clear, the best line becomes much easier to find.
