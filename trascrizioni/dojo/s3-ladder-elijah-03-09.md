# OP17 MIHAWK LADDER SESSION - ELIJAH

### Session Overview

This ladder session is Elijah working through the practical reality of OP17 Mihawk rather than presenting a fixed final guide. The main focus is the unresolved choice between the Law plus Yasopp build and the Mihawk plus Perona build, with live games used to evaluate mirror performance, Enel, Robin, and a range of fringe ladder opponents.

His starting conclusion is nuanced:

- The Law plus Yasopp build appears better into the wider field.
- The Mihawk plus Perona build appears better in the mirror.
- Elijah is personally leaning slightly toward Law plus Yasopp, but sees the decision as close.
- The exact final 50 is still unsettled.
- The deciding factor is expected field composition, especially how many Mihawk mirrors and Mihawk plus Perona lists are expected at the top tables.

The session also sharpens several practical conclusions:

- Three Vander Deckens are probably enough.
- Three Boats are mandatory.
- Law/Bepo has been underwhelming in the mirror specifically.
- Stun Bonney is powerful in theory, but Elijah is personally less impressed with it than many other top players.
- Luna is much better against Mihawk plus Perona boards than against Law plus Yasopp boards.
- Perona proved much better in practice than Elijah expected in several non-mirror games.
- Enel is meaningfully harder for Mihawk now than it was in OP16.5, especially if Enel is built to target Mihawk.

## THE TWO MIHAWK BUILDS

### Law plus Yasopp

Elijah’s working view is that Law plus Yasopp is the stronger all-field version.

The build’s advantages:

- Yasopp provides board control against smaller characters.
- Yasopp is difficult to punish because it can rest for leader effect and then stand again.
- Law gives flexible double-development turns.
- Law can recycle a Perona or Vander Decken.
- Law creates blocker bodies.
- Law can be played later in the game more naturally than Mihawk.
- Law helps the deck make meaningful plays after the first Shanks turn.
- Yasopp makes several difficult matchups more manageable, especially where early board control matters.

The Law build’s weaknesses:

- It is more dependent on having enough one-drops.
- Samurai can be harder to resolve because the deck cannot afford to lose all of its one-drops.
- It can fail if it cannot Law, Samurai, or pick up a relevant body.
- It is less naturally powerful in the mirror because its main bodies are smaller.
- It needs more careful management of one-drop count, counter, and board space.

Elijah repeatedly notes that the Law version can develop “fail rate” when it does not have enough one-drops to Samurai or pick up with Law. This is one reason he does not want to cut too many small bodies or 2ks in the shell.

### Mihawk plus Perona

The Mihawk plus Perona build is the mirror-focused option.

Its advantages:

- Mihawk has a 7k stat line.
- Perona is excellent in the mirror.
- Mihawk is harder to trade into than smaller Law bodies.
- Perona can float into a new character.
- The build can create high-value double development through Mihawk plus Perona.
- It performs better against a mirror field where opponents play Law plus Yasopp.
- Perona gives strong board pressure and better resilience in games where it lives for multiple turns.

The Mihawk plus Perona build’s weaknesses:

- It is less flexible into the broad field.
- It gives up some Yasopp-based board control.
- It can be less comfortable against Robin, Enel, and other decks where Yasopp’s immediate board interaction matters.
- It has fewer natural ways to exploit Stun Bonney with Law recursion.
- It may lose equity into the decks designed specifically to challenge Mihawk.

Elijah’s evaluation is not that one shell is unquestionably better. It is: Law plus Yasopp is likely better into the field. Mihawk plus Perona is likely better into the mirror.

The correct submission depends on whether the event is expected to be a Mihawk-heavy top-table tournament or a broad field full of Enel, Robin, Sabo, Kaido, and rogue decks.

## DECKLIST FINDINGS

### Three Vander Deckens

Elijah is firmly leaning toward three Vander Deckens.

Why three is enough:

- The deck has huge card velocity.
- You usually only need to resolve one Vander Decken in a game.
- Opponents now play around Decken better than they did in early OP17 testing.
- The old lines where Decken repeatedly punished blockers or major bodies are less common.
- A fourth copy can become a brick.
- The deck has better uses for its limited flex slots.

The old high-value Decken pattern was:

1. Opponent blocks with a key body.
2. Mihawk plays Decken and removes it.
3. Next turn, Mihawk plays Shanks and Decken again.
4. Opponent loses multiple major bodies while Mihawk keeps pushing.

Elijah believes competent opponents now prevent this pattern more often. Therefore, three copies provide enough access without overloading the deck.

### Three Boats Are Mandatory

Elijah is very clear that he wants three Boats.

Boat matters because it:

- Provides a reliable rest target.
- Supports Samurai.
- Improves stage and event conversion.
- Helps the deck cycle and maintain card flow.
- Makes later turns less awkward.
- Allows Mihawk to use events without sacrificing the ability to develop and attack.
- Gives the deck a more stable structure in games where it needs to hold defensive DON!!.

He does not want to play fewer than three Boats.

### Shanks Count

Elijah’s current list was playing three Shanks, which made him nervous in some ladder games.

With four Shanks:

- It is easy to pitch an early Shanks through Kin’emon.
- You do not need to take Shanks off searches as often.
- Missing Shanks is less concerning.
- The deck has more redundancy in its primary finisher.

With three Shanks:

- Pitching an early Shanks feels much riskier.
- You may need to hold a Shanks that would otherwise be a reasonable discard.
- The deck can fail to assemble its key 10-DON!! turns.
- Search priorities become more awkward.

Elijah was not fully settled, but the session shows the practical cost of lowering Shanks count: the deck becomes less willing to use Shanks as discard fuel and less able to trust its deck velocity.

### Stun Bonney

Elijah is personally lower on Stun Bonney than much of the ladder.

Why he is skeptical:

- Bonney stops the opponent’s 10-DON!! bomb turn, but does not stop their current board from attacking.
- If the opponent has not been board-controlled, they can still send four or five attacks.
- Stun Bonney can leave the Mihawk player taking too much pressure even while preventing Shanks.
- Against Mihawk plus Perona, the opponent may still have a functional Perona turn and enough board to maintain pressure.
- The card often feels like an excellent 1k counter rather than a decisive win condition.

He sees cases where it is strong:

- If Yasopp has already removed important opposing bodies.
- If the opponent’s board is reduced to only a few threats.
- If Bonney forces the opponent into an awkward Perona-only turn.
- If Law can recycle Bonney and deny multiple bomb turns.
- Into yellow decks where denying the first 10-DON!! Big Mom turn is genuinely devastating.

But he does not believe Bonney alone automatically wins the mirror.

### Luna Versus Stronger

Elijah’s view depends on which mirror shell dominates.

If the expected mirror is **Law plus Yasopp**:

- Stronger can be reasonable.
- The board is often narrower.
- Luna may not freeze enough targets to justify itself.
- Stronger is better outside the mirror because its counter text matters more often.

If the expected mirror is **Mihawk plus Perona**:

- Luna is much better.
- Perona floats into additional bodies.
- Mihawk’s 7k bodies are harder to clear.
- The opponent can easily have four or more relevant targets.
- Stronger is too small against that board.
- Luna is required to prevent the opponent’s full board from converting.

Elijah is worried that the Mihawk plus Perona build will remain common enough that cutting Luna entirely is dangerous.

### One-Cost Zoro

Elijah discusses one-cost Zoro as an unusual flex option.

Potential uses:

- Strong against Sabo before its smaller bodies awaken.
- Can punish a weak Law build mirror opener if the opponent has only one one-drop.
- Has some merit against Enel.
- Can sometimes completely swing an early board race.

Why he is unsure:

- There is only one early Sabo turn where it is truly high-impact.
- It is not something the deck mulligans for.
- Law lists often play enough one-drops to make the line rare.
- Mihawk plus Perona lists may not care as much.
- It is not reliable enough to be a core slot.

Elijah sees it as a possible meta call, not a standard inclusion.

## LAW/BEPO FINDINGS

### Underwhelming in the Mirror

One of Elijah’s clearest ladder findings is that Law/Bepo has been underwhelming in the mirror.

Why:

- The opponent can Law/Bepo and still needs Luna.
- If the opponent does not Luna, Mihawk can attack into their board repeatedly.
- Law/Bepo often dies quickly.
- It does not create the same board-locking pressure as Shanks plus Decken plus Luna.
- It may gain only one extra 1k or one extra swing, which often does not decide the game.
- The deck can be better served by Shanks and Luna setups.

Elijah’s central question is: What does Law/Bepo actually do that Shanks plus Vander Decken plus Luna does not already do better?

He currently sees Law/Bepo as potentially better outside the mirror than inside it, but he is not yet convinced it is necessary.

### Possible Non-Mirror Value

Law/Bepo may still have merit outside the mirror because it:

- Raises leader power.
- Punishes players who turn their full board sideways to play around Shanks.
- Buys an extra turn.
- Can invalidate a board of 6k attackers.
- Can serve as a late-game bridge between first and second Shanks.

Elijah does not rule it out. He simply sees far less mirror impact than expected.

## MIHAWK MIRROR FINDINGS

### The Mirror Is About Pressure and Counter Density

The ladder session reinforces that the mirror is not only about bomb timing. It is about using 7k attacks to strain a deck with limited 2k density.

Elijah repeatedly favors attack patterns such as:

- 7k, 7k, 7k
- 7k, 7k, 7k, 7k
- 8k, 8k when the opponent has no relevant open DON!!
- 12k when the opponent is already low and cannot efficiently defend

Why 7k matters:

- The deck often plays limited 2ks.
- Many 1ks are placed on board or used for engine effects.
- The opponent may need to give several 2ks to stop a sequence of 7k attacks.
- Repeated 7k attacks can drain the opponent’s hand faster than mixed 6k/8k lines.

Elijah’s goal is often to “2k-check” the opponent repeatedly rather than offer a sequence they can answer with one event, one small counter, or a free block.

### Perona Is Better Than It Looks

Elijah came away impressed with Perona.

In one Enel game, Perona remained in play and attacked several times because Enel could not clear it cleanly. Elijah noted that if Perona had been any other 5-cost body, it would have been removed immediately.

Perona’s value:

- It floats into a new body when removed.
- It creates repeated attacks if ignored.
- It gives a standing body that helps preserve board density.
- It contributes meaningful board pressure in matchups where the opponent cannot afford to trade into it.

This finding makes the Mihawk plus Perona shell more attractive than its abstract theory suggests.

### Stun Timing

Elijah gives a concise theory of stuns:

> Stunning is only good in three situations.

1. The stun prevents the opponent from winning before your lethal next turn.
2. You will continue stunning the board until you win.
3. You stun the board now so you can kill it next turn.

Stunning too early is often wasted value. The opponent untaps, and the body attacks later anyway.

Before playing Luna, Stronger, Perona, Oden, or another stun, ask:

- Does this prevent lethal now?
- Does this create lethal next turn?
- Does this let me remove the target before it attacks again?
- Does this buy enough time to reach Shanks?
- Will the opponent simply untap and continue their plan?

If none of these are true, the stun may be a tempo-negative action.

### Board Versus Face

Elijah’s mirror decisions are based on projected game length, not merely the number of rested bodies.

When choosing between attacking board and face, ask:

- Who is ahead on board?
- Who is ahead on hand?
- Does one player need to race?
- Can the opponent stabilize if given another turn?
- Does clearing a body remove an actual future attack?
- Does attacking life put the opponent into Shanks range?
- Is the game likely to end in one, two, or three turns?

The number of rested bodies alone should not decide the line.

### Samurai Timing

Elijah becomes more cautious about Samurai on turn three.

Why Samurai on three can be bad:

- You often have only a few one-drops.
- The opponent can trade into those one-drops.
- You may lose access to Law development.
- You may leave yourself too low on bodies for future Samurai.
- A Perona can be especially painful to expose because it is one of the more valuable one-drops.

Samurai is more attractive on turn four when:

- You have more one-drops in play.
- You can replace them with new standing bodies.
- You can develop two characters afterward.
- You can trade into an opposing body first, then Samurai.
- The opponent has limited ability to trade back.

Elijah’s practical advice:

- Do not automatically Samurai on three.
- Samurai on three is reasonable if the opponent has zero or one one-drop.
- If the opponent has several one-drops, preserve bodies for Law and future development.
- Consider attacking into a one-drop first, then Samurai once the board is safer.

## MIHAWK VS ENEL

### Matchup Assessment

Elijah finds Enel harder now than in OP16.5 when playing the Law plus Yasopp version.

Why:

- Enel is more specifically built to beat Mihawk.
- Enel has X-Drake, Law, and other interactions.
- Enel can hand-rip key pieces.
- Enel can open many one-drops, especially Ohm.
- Enel can use Mamaragan to gain life and preserve hand.
- Enel can force Mihawk to protect board while still keeping a wide engine.

This is no longer a matchup Mihawk can treat as routine. It requires tighter counter management and better recognition of Enel’s board texture.

### Early-Life Decisions

Elijah is willing to take some early hits, but he specifically thinks about the future swing distribution.

A useful question is: Will there be enough attacks above 6k before my 10-DON!! turn that taking this 6k now is actually dangerous?

If the answer is no, taking can be correct.

If future attacks are mostly 6k or smaller:

- You may simply counter later with the same card.
- Taking life now may give Perona, Kin’emon, stage, or another playable.
- You gain hand quality and more options.

However, when Enel has a wide one-drop board and repeated 6k attacks, Mihawk must be more careful. The total number of swings can become more dangerous than the individual attack size.

### Do Not Give Free Mamaragan

A major Enel lesson from the session: Do not attack Enel for 5k if you have unused DON!! and no reason to keep it open.

A 5k attack can be Mamaragan’d efficiently.

When Enel Mamaragans a 5k:

- Enel keeps hand parity.
- Enel gains a life.
- That life later absorbs a larger 7k attack.
- Mihawk’s pressure is effectively reduced by much more than the current 5k attack.

If Mihawk is not using the DON!! for:

- Samurai
- Boat
- search
- counter events
- development

then it should increase the attack size to 6k or 7k and force a more expensive response.

### Triple Ohm Problem

Elijah is very clear that multiple Ohms change the matchup.

Against triple Ohm:

- Your medium bodies are often dying no matter what.
- Trying to clear every Ohm can be futile.
- Enel can trade back into most small bodies.
- Samurai can become correct even with larger bodies because those bodies are doomed anyway.
- The right plan may be to convert bodies into cards before Ohm trades consume them.

In the session, Elijah says that he would normally avoid Samurai’ing large bodies, but against a board like triple Ohm, he may Samurai them because they are unlikely to survive either way.

### Hand-Rip Law

Elijah is willing to play around hand-rip Law if his hand has a critical vulnerability.

Counter early if:

- The opponent’s Law can take the only two cards that make your next turn function.
- You have one Oden and one key 5-cost, and losing both destroys your curve.
- You lack redundancy.
- You are already low on life and cannot afford another pressure turn.

Do not overplay around Law if your hand is redundant. Losing an Oden and Mihawk is bad, but not necessarily worth giving up too much life or hand if you still have functional follow-up.

### Perona and Yasopp

Elijah sees Perona as excellent against Enel because it is hard to cleanly remove.

Yasopp is good when:

- It can remove Enel’s key midrange character.
- It prevents Enel from sticking a body and then building another 4-cost behind it.
- It lets Mihawk control the snowball point before Enel gets too wide.

The general priority is:

- Control Enel’s one-drops where possible.
- Remove midrange bodies before they snowball.
- Do not expect your board to remain untouched.
- Use Shanks and Luna to create a final turn where Enel’s one-drops no longer attack.

### Enel Win Condition for Mihawk

Elijah’s expected winning sequence is often:

1. Survive early pressure without losing all hand.
2. Use Perona, Yasopp, Oden, and Law to reduce Enel’s board.
3. Push Enel low enough for Shanks.
4. Play Shanks plus Luna.
5. Disable the one-drop board.
6. Force Enel to spend resources without being able to attack back.
7. Use the second Shanks to close.

## MIHAWK VS ROBIN

### Matchup Assessment

Elijah prefers going first into Robin, particularly when using the Law plus Yasopp shell.

Why:

- Going first makes Robin’s strongest ramp start harder.
- Mihawk gets an extra attacker before Robin can fully stabilize.
- The Mihawk player can pressure Robin before double ramp, Teach, and Big Mom become active.
- Robin’s high-roll line is dangerous if Mihawk goes second and fails to establish early board.

Robin’s strongest start is approximately:

1. Go second.
2. Double ramp.
3. Play Teach on 6 DON!! turn.
4. Play Big Mom afterward.
5. Build into a healing, board-control shell.

If Robin executes this while Mihawk misses six-cost development and small bodies, the matchup can become extremely difficult.

### Why Law Plus Yasopp Helps

Elijah thinks Robin is harder without Law plus Yasopp.

Yasopp helps because:

- It removes Robin’s 4k bodies.
- It reduces Robin’s board before Linlin enters.
- It can prevent Robin from getting multiple attackers.
- It keeps Robin’s first Big Mom turn from converting enough pressure.
- It gives Mihawk a way to control board while still developing.

Law helps because:

- It can replay Perona.
- It can develop blockers.
- It can reuse Vander Decken.
- It creates more relevant late-game plays.
- It helps Mihawk go wide enough that Robin cannot survive solely through defensive cards.

### Attack Plan

Against Robin, Elijah wants to attack for real numbers, especially when low-rolling.

If the DON!! is not needed elsewhere:

- Do not settle for a 5k attack into a possible Teach trigger.
- Consider whether 6k or 7k creates more useful pressure.
- Use board pressure to force Robin to spend cards before its healing engine comes online.
- Go wide rather than rely entirely on Shanks.

Elijah specifically notes that Mihawk may prefer 6-cost plus 5-cost development repeatedly over rushing Shanks. Robin struggles when Mihawk reaches a board of multiple attackers and can present five or more swings.

### When Robin Damages Mihawk With Characters

Elijah identifies a major Robin mistake: attacking Mihawk’s life with characters too aggressively.

Why it is risky:

- Mihawk can take the damage.
- Taking life gives Mihawk more cards.
- Robin may fail to trade into Mihawk’s board.
- Mihawk’s Laws become blockers.
- Robin’s board is not actually reducing Mihawk’s future board.
- Mihawk can become too wide for Robin to answer.

Robin needs to be careful. If it attacks life but does not remove board, it may simply give Mihawk the hand it needs to assemble Shanks, Decken, Luna, and Law turns.

### Robin High-Roll Warning

Even though Elijah prefers Mihawk, he is clear that Robin can create an almost unwinnable start.

The dangerous situation is:

- Robin double ramps.
- Robin plays Teach on curve.
- Mihawk misses a 6-cost development.
- Mihawk fails to find enough one-drops.
- Samurai gets stuck in hand.
- Robin hits trigger value.
- Big Mom comes down before Mihawk establishes a board.

In those games, Mihawk can lose not because the macro plan is wrong, but because Robin has combined acceleration, hand disruption, trigger value, and a major healing threat before Mihawk is online.

## MIHAWK VERSUS FIELDS WITH LOW COUNTER

### Attack for Sevens

Elijah repeatedly returns to the importance of knowing the opponent’s counter distribution.

Against decks with limited 2k density:

- Attack for 7k repeatedly.
- Force them to give 2ks or events.
- Do not default to 6k if 7k is available.
- Do not overcommit into a giant swing if multiple 7ks are more difficult to defend.
- Use the deck’s free DON!! to make every medium body ask a real question.

He calls this “2k-checking.”

The goal is to create a turn where the opponent needs more 2ks than their deck is likely to have.

## GAMEPLAY FINDINGS

### When to Leave DON!! Open

Elijah’s rule is not “always leave DON!! for events.”

Leave DON!! open when:

- You need an event to survive the opponent’s likely attack spread.
- You need Rad Beam to protect a key body.
- The event will become uncastable on the next all-DON!! Shanks turn.
- You are preparing for a known 7k, 7k, 7k attack sequence.
- You need to preserve a specific life total before a bomb turn.

Do not leave DON!! open merely because you want the opponent to counter. If the DON!! is unused and a larger attack prevents Mamaragan, forces a 2k, or advances lethal, attach it.

### When to Protect a Body

Elijah’s practical framework:

Protect the body when:

- It will attack again.
- It keeps you wide enough that the opponent must use Luna.
- It creates a future 8k attack.
- It lets you maintain an attacker advantage.
- It prevents the opponent from getting a clean board-control line.
- It is a key blocker or engine body.
- You have enough card flow to afford the counter investment.

Let the body die when:

- It gets one weak attack and then enters Luna jail.
- You need the hand to survive a Shanks turn.
- The body is not needed for lethal.
- The opponent can still remove it with multiple attacks.
- Your remaining board is already wide enough.
- It cannot generate more than one card of value before becoming irrelevant.

### Lethal Planning

Elijah’s late-game plan is often:

1. Put the opponent at one or zero.
2. Shanks plus Luna.
3. Skip their meaningful turn.
4. Use second Shanks or remaining board to finish.

He emphasizes that players should calculate whether the game ends next turn or requires another turn.

If the game ends next turn:

- Use Luna aggressively.
- Spend the Shanks turn to disable the board.
- Do not preserve resources unnecessarily.

If the game requires more than two turns:

- Do not waste all Lunas early.
- Preserve events.
- Consider board control instead of forcing a low-percentage lethal.
- Use Law, Perona, or Oden to buy time and build toward the second Shanks.

## FINAL TAKEAWAYS

**Deck selection**

- Law plus Yasopp is likely better into the field.
- Mihawk plus Perona is likely better in the mirror.
- Elijah slightly leans Law plus Yasopp but remains undecided.
- The final choice should depend on expected mirror density and tournament field composition.

**List construction**

- Three Boats are mandatory.
- Three Vander Deckens are likely enough.
- Three Shanks can create awkward discard decisions; four may be safer.
- Stun Bonney is strong in theory but not automatically correct.
- Luna is necessary if Mihawk plus Perona boards are common.
- Stronger becomes more attractive only if Law plus Yasopp mirrors dominate.
- Law/Bepo is currently underwhelming in the mirror but may retain non-mirror value.

**Gameplay**

- Do not Samurai blindly on turn three.
- Preserve one-drops for Law and future Samurai turns.
- Use 7k attacks to 2k-check opponents.
- Do not give Enel free Mamaragan on 5k attacks.
- Take early life when future attack math permits it.
- Counter early when hand-rip Law can remove your only functional curve.
- Use Perona more aggressively than abstract theory may suggest.
- Do not let Robin establish its full ramp, Teach, Big Mom sequence uncontested.
- Know whether your board is actually worth protecting before spending the counter.

**Tournament view**

Mihawk remains one of OP17’s strongest decks because it rewards card tracking, matchup knowledge, disciplined attack sizing, and correct late-game planning. The player who identifies the opponent’s counter thresholds, maps a two-Shanks kill, and uses their one-drops and searchers efficiently will gain more from the deck than a player simply copying the strongest list.
