# «Mihawk+Perona vs Law+Yasopp» — LawSopp vs HawkRona (documento comparativo)

Trascrizione fedele del documento `LawSopp vs HawkRona.docx` (fonte in `fonti/`, non versionata).
Confronta le due versioni del Green Mihawk OP17 argomento per argomento:

- **Sam: HawkRona** — 6c Mihawk + Perona
- **Impact: LawSopp** — Law + Yasopp

Ogni riga: la posizione di Sam, la posizione di Impact, e la colonna "What it means in practice".

| Topic | Sam · HawkRona | Impact · LawSopp | What it means in practice |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary deck identity | Aggressive midrange tuned for consistency, clean resource conversion, and mirror performance | Aggressive midrange tuned for board development, tempo, and rapid Shanks finishing | Both are proactive decks. Neither teacher believes OP17 Mihawk should primarily play as a slow control deck. |
| Central philosophy | Reduce failed hands and reach the Shanks pivot with the right cards, board state, life total, and counter resources | Build a board quickly, use extra DON to attack efficiently, and force the opponent into Shanks kill range | Sam favors a higher floor; Impact emphasizes a higher tempo ceiling. |
| Preferred 5/6-cost package | 6-cost Mihawk and Perona | Trafalgar Law and Yasopp, with Perona searcher as a reusable engine | This is the main structural difference between the lists. |
| Why play 6c Mihawk? | It works independently, filters a card, can replace bricks, and produces a more stable development turn | Not the preferred centerpiece; Impact favors Law's ability to double-develop and recycle utility bodies | Sam sees filtering as a major form of card advantage in a deck with several non-counter, high-cost cards. |
| Why play Law? | Law can be strong but is viewed as dependent on a good opener and surviving small bodies | Law is a core engine: pick up Perona/searchers, develop another major body, and keep chaining value | The disagreement is whether Law's setup cost is a meaningful liability or an intended, manageable part of the deck's engine. |
| Why play Yasopp? | Strong card, especially against Sabo, but not enough payoff by itself to justify Law over Mihawk in a mirror-focused field | One of the deck's most important cards: early board control, resilient attacker, Law target, and Shanks support | If you value early board interaction highly, Impact's evaluation of Yasopp becomes much more compelling. |
| Yasopp's self-restand | Helpful, but not viewed as decisive in the mirror | Central to the build: Yasopp can be developed rested through Law yet restand, making the Law turn less vulnerable | Impact's LawSopp construction specifically solves the "Law leaves a body sideways" problem by making Yasopp the preferred deployment. |
| Perona's role (1c/5c) | Freeze, disruption, and a way to make meaningful turns when Shanks is unavailable or delayed | Extremely important searcher that should often be bounced by Law to find continuation pieces | Both highly value Perona. Sam emphasizes her independent freeze/filter utility; Impact emphasizes repeated access through Law. |
| Perona + Law interaction | Recognized as good card flow, but Sam believes it does not outweigh Mihawk's independent filtering and board quality | A signature line: bounce Perona with Law and play a 5c, replay her, and look for Shanks, Luna, events, or follow-up plays | LawSopp gains ceiling and deeper digging when it can repeatedly convert Perona. |
| Brick management | 6c Mihawk is preferred because it can actively filter a brick out of hand | Use Samurai, Coffin Boat, Kinemon, leader-generated DON, and Law/Perona loops to avoid getting clogged | Both acknowledge green Mihawk has bricks. Sam relies more on built-in filtering; Impact relies more on active card cycling and sequencing. |
| View of raw card draw | Drawing an extra card is not always better if the original brick remains in your hand | High draw density lets the deck find otherwise unsearchable pieces and maintain follow-up | Sam differentiates "more cards" from "better cards"; Impact values the volume of looks generated by the deck's engines. |
| Opener dependence | Low-to-moderate: Mihawk still functions if the opponent clears early characters | Higher: Law is best when there is a worthwhile body to pick up and convert | Sam's version is more resilient when early searchers die or when the opening hand is uneven. |
| Small-body vulnerability | Less reliant on small bodies remaining alive before the 5/6-cost turn | Needs early bodies to maximize Law, although the deck can develop many of them and convert them into value | Against decks that efficiently remove 1-costs, Sam's structural argument gets stronger. |
| Samurai interaction | Samurai remains an important source of advantage; Sam dislikes that Law can make Samurai lines awkward | Samurai remains central, but Impact's guide emphasizes using excess DON to convert Samurai and avoid bricking | Sam sees potential anti-synergy: Law wants a body to bounce, while Samurai can expose/remove the bodies Law needs. |
| Early-game objective | Establish draw/search, preserve resources, and prepare the correct curve into the mirror/Shanks pivot | Draw cards, search for curve pieces, take early life when appropriate, and set up future development | Both prioritize card access early; Impact is more explicit about taking life to acquire combo pieces. |
| Midgame objective | Develop enough pressure while preserving the correct hand, counter thresholds, and board state for Shanks | Establish 6K attackers, convert them into 7K swings with free DON, and pressure life every turn | Sam is more cautious about resource structure; Impact is more explicit about relentless tempo conversion. |
| Use of extra DON | Spend it where it advances life mapping or allows important defensive/event management; avoid wasting it on low-value development | Use it aggressively to push damage, play searchers/Samurai, cycle bricks, and maintain board pressure | Both recognize free DON as Mihawk's defining resource advantage. Impact pushes harder toward immediate damage. |
| Attacking philosophy | In the mirror, attack sizing and life preservation must be designed around the Shanks race | Attack aggressively, especially with 7K lines, to force the opponent into Shanks kill range | Sam's attacks are more mirror-clock-focused; Impact's attacks are more universally pressure-focused. |
| Preferred board state | Stable, active bodies and a hand that can execute a Shanks turn without collapsing to counter pressure | Wide board of sticky 6K bodies that can repeatedly swing for 7K and force life/cards | Sam prefers robustness; Impact wants density of attackers. |
| Board protection | Important, but Sam's analysis values whether the board actually survives the opponent's Shanks/Luna pivot | A major priority: midgame 6K bodies are how the deck converts wins, and Billionfold should often protect them | Impact places particular weight on preserving 6K attackers because they become the bridge to Shanks lethal. |
| Shanks evaluation | The defining bomb of the mirror: the 12K rush swing changes life thresholds and forces careful countering | "Does everything": finisher, removal, setup for removal, setup for Luna, and a fast closing mechanism | Both see Shanks as the reason OP17 Mihawk's endgame is dramatically stronger than earlier versions. |
| Shanks timing | Do not delay the relevant Shanks turn in the mirror; if you wait, the opponent can resolve theirs first | Deploy Shanks in the late game and often aim to finish by the second 10-DON turn | Both want timely Shanks; Sam stresses race math, while Impact stresses converting the midgame into a fast close. |
| Shanks + Luna | A decisive mirror sequence because Shanks can make the opponent's board vulnerable to Luna/freeze effects | A primary tempo tool: freeze the opposing board while Shanks pushes damage and prepares lethal | Both see this pairing as powerful; Sam discusses it more as mirror interaction, Impact more as a general finishing pattern. |
| Shanks + Vander Decken | Key answer to high-cost bombs and an important reason Kaido has become less attractive | A major matchup tool for dealing with 7–8+ DON threats while maintaining your own pressure | Both treat Decken as a premium answer to large characters, not merely a generic removal card. |
| Law & Bepo | A compact "second bomb" that can replace a complicated multi-card defensive sequence and buy an extra turn | Usually a one-of extension tool; useful when one extra turn is needed but no longer the deck's main identity | Sam gives Law & Bepo more strategic weight in the mirror; Impact treats it as a powerful but secondary extension card. |
| Role of freezes | Freeze effects are central to interrupting an opposing Shanks turn or reducing a board's conversion | Freezes are for tempo, not prolonged control; use them to buy the turn needed to kill | This is mostly agreement. Neither wants to freeze merely to extend a game indefinitely. |
| View of long control games | Avoid them when possible; the build is designed to win the critical bomb turns efficiently | Explicitly rejects OP14-style long Law & Bepo control loops as the deck's main plan | Strong agreement: OP17 Mihawk should finish, not endlessly stall. |
| Mirror priority | Highest deck-building priority; Sam expects Mihawk to be common enough that mirror equity should shape flex slots | Important matchup, but Impact's guide focuses on executing the core pressure curve and damage map | Sam is more willing to sacrifice some non-mirror edge for mirror tools and consistency. |
| Mirror going second | Very desirable because the second player can establish the more favorable double-development/Shanks sequence | Also says to go second; follow the normal curve and use early draw to secure counter and bomb access | Strong agreement: going second is preferred in the mirror. |
| Mirror life management | You often need to preserve at least two life before the opponent's Shanks turn because dropping to 0 is extremely dangerous | Counter smaller swings early because Shanks attacks can become too large to defend efficiently | Sam gives more exact conceptual weight to life thresholds; Impact gives practical attack-size guidance. |
| Mirror attack thresholds | Focus is on putting the opponent to 0 first while retaining enough counter to survive retaliation | Recommends 7K frequently, 8K when no DON is up, 9K when 8 rested cards are unavailable, and 10K to attack through Billionfold | Impact gives concrete breakpoints for the mirror. |
| Mirror tech: Stun Bonney | Major innovation in Sam's approach; can deny the opposing Shanks turn and reverse who gets the first bomb pivot | Not a central element of Impact's published standard list or matchup plan | This is a substantial practical distinction. Sam sees Bonney as a way to fix going-first mirror weakness. |
| View of 7c/9c Shanks lines | Less attractive because Vander Decken interacts favorably and the newer freeze environment reduces their value | Focus remains on 10c Shanks as the major endgame bomb | Sam is more explicit that Decken has narrowed the usefulness of prior Shanks options. |
| Sabo matchup | Acknowledges Yasopp meaningfully improves the matchup, but does not believe that alone outweighs HawkRona's mirror advantages | Identifies Sabo as one of the more difficult matchups and builds specifically to answer it through Yasopp, Oden, Decken, and careful countering | Sabo is Impact's strongest practical argument for LawSopp. |
| Sabo plan | Attack proactively rather than attempting a full starvation strategy; selectively attack rested bodies when offered | Use Yasopp to clear small bodies, preserve Decken for Luffy/Loki, freeze high-impact units with Oden, and aim for a two-turn Shanks kill | Sam is more skeptical of trying to completely control Sabo; Impact leans more into selective board control. |
| Sabo and Yasopp | Yasopp's key benefit is turning an opponent's unit sideways so you can contest it while retaining your own active body | Yasopp is the early answer that prevents Sabo's low-cost value bodies from snowballing | Both agree Yasopp is good into Sabo; they differ on how much that should determine the full list. |
| Kaido matchup | Shanks + Vander Decken materially improves the matchup; Decken is a major reason Kaido is less appealing into Mihawk | Also emphasizes Shanks + Decken as a huge tempo exchange; Yasopp can help restand Shanks and attack safely | Both see Decken as critical. Sam leans toward consistent access; Impact emphasizes on-board execution. |
| Kaido attack sequencing | Prioritize avoiding inefficient exchanges and maintaining the bomb-turn plan | Attack with 6K characters at 7K to play around leader reduction; use leader DON differently when setting up lethal | Impact provides more specific tactical sequencing guidance. |
| Robin matchup | Stun Bonney and freeze-related options can incidentally improve yellow/mom board turns | Yasopp clears 4K units; pressure early so Robin cannot freely establish two Big Mom turns; use Shanks + Decken/Luna to remove key bodies | Impact's guide gives a detailed Robin plan. |
| Rocks matchup | Mihawk is naturally strong; Law & Bepo can be an alternate method of invalidating their board | Use Luna heavily, account for Captain John stall, pressure with appropriate attack sizes, and respect Shiki | Both are optimistic about the matchup. |
| Enel matchup | Preserve appropriate life and prepare Shanks turns; freezes can limit their board conversion | Use low-cost bodies to clear 1-costs, push life with leader, reserve Oden for large Enels, and avoid losing more than one life per turn | Impact's guide covers defense and combo timing against Enel. *(Nota della guida: Sam non tratta Enel nel video — la colonna Sam qui è un'estrapolazione dell'autore del documento.)* |
| Defensive events | Be highly intentional about leaving DON up, especially because full Shanks/Luna turns consume most available DON | Generally spend around one DON per turn for events when appropriate, but calculate actual incoming swings and weave events out before they clog | Both agree event management is a real skill test in the deck. |
| Coffin Boat | Sam is lower on high event/Boat density than older builds because newer bomb turns frequently consume DON and some events matter less | Impact explicitly includes and uses Coffin Boat as part of the deck's card-flow and brick-management infrastructure | This is a secondary but meaningful deck-construction difference. *(Nota della guida: sul motivo di Sam vedi la ⚠️ Coffin Boat in Deck → Scelte di lista — nel video il ragionamento è diverso.)* |
| 2K counter philosophy | Strongly values ordinary 2K counter density because many mirror attacks are one-card hits | Emphasizes managing the deck's brick count and using counter events/resources without becoming clogged | Sam's version leans toward reliable middle-game defense; Impact focuses more on actively converting awkward cards. |
| Main source of advantage | Card quality, clean sequencing, filtering, freeze timing, and mirror disruption | Card volume, repeated searches, double development, resilient Yasopp pressure, and free-DON attacks | Sam improves quality of resources; Impact increases quantity and tempo of resource conversion. |
| Main failure mode | Potentially gives up some direct board-control edge, especially into Sabo, by not fully exploiting Yasopp | Can draw the wrong mix of setup cards/bricks, lose Law targets, or face awkward Samurai/Law sequencing | Pick Sam's list if you hate inconsistency; pick Impact's list if you hate losing the board early. |
| Skill test | Life mapping, counter thresholds, identifying when to skip lower-value development, and navigating the mirror bomb race | Sequencing searches before Law, Law target management, board protection, identifying damage breakpoints, and planning multistep lethal turns | Both are difficult decks, but their difficult decisions occur at different points in the game. |
| Best player profile | Player who wants consistency, anticipates many Mihawk mirrors, and prefers a disciplined, calculated bomb-turn plan | Player who enjoys active board combat, technical sequencing, repeated Perona loops, and applying pressure every turn | The "better" list may be less about raw power than about your local field and decision-making strengths. |
| Best metagame profile | Mihawk-heavy, open, uncertain, or interaction-heavy field where clean execution is paramount | Sabo-heavy, small-body-heavy, board-centric field where Yasopp can repeatedly produce tempo | Sam's build hedges against expected mirrors; Impact's build attacks established boards. |
| Most transferable lesson | Do not confuse drawing more cards with improving hand quality; life/counter math determines whether Shanks actually wins | Do not play Mihawk passively; build the board, convert free DON into 7K pressure, and map the opponent into Shanks range | Combining these lessons produces the strongest overall Mihawk fundamentals. |
